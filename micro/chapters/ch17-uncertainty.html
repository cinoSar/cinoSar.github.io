The Problem: Should You Buy Travel Insurance?

You are booking a £3,000 holiday. The airline offers travel insurance for £80. After reading the small print, you estimate a 5% chance of having to cancel, in which case you would lose the entire £3,000. The expected loss is therefore 0.05 × £3,000 = £150 — almost twice the £80 premium. On purely expected-value grounds, the insurance looks like a bargain.

But now suppose the insurer offers a cheaper policy at £40 — below the expected loss. Would you still buy it? Most people would. And a strictly expected-value-maximising agent should too, since EV(insure) > EV(do not insure). But what if the premium were £200 — above the expected loss? A strict expected-value maximiser would refuse. Yet many people still buy. And what explains why someone would pay £80 for insurance when the expected loss is only £60? Something richer than expected monetary value is at work.

The resolution lies in expected utility theory. People do not maximise expected monetary value; they maximise the expected utility of wealth. Because the marginal utility of money diminishes, losing £3,000 causes far more disutility than a 5% chance of losing £3,000 implies on average. A risk-averse agent will pay a premium above the expected loss to avoid uncertainty.

Key Insight

Rational agents with diminishing marginal utility of wealth are risk averse: they strictly prefer a certain outcome to a fair gamble with the same expected value. The maximum amount they would pay to eliminate a risk — the risk premium — is determined by the curvature of their utility function. Insurance, diversification, and hedging all exploit this willingness to pay for certainty.

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to explain why expected value maximisation is an inadequate model of choice under uncertainty, apply expected utility theory to rank lotteries and evaluate insurance decisions, derive the certainty equivalent and risk premium for a given utility function, use the state-contingent consumption diagram to show why full insurance is optimal when premiums are actuarially fair, and explain how diversification reduces portfolio risk by exploiting imperfect correlation between assets.

17.1 Expected Value and Expected Utility

Intuition: The St Petersburg Paradox

In 1713, Nicolaus Bernoulli posed a puzzle that stumped mathematicians for decades. A casino flips a fair coin repeatedly until heads appears. If heads comes on the first flip, you win £2; on the second, £4; on the third, £8; and so on — the prize doubles each round. How much should a rational person pay to play?

The expected value of the game is \(\frac{1}{2} \cdot 2 + \frac{1}{4} \cdot 4 + \frac{1}{8} \cdot 8 + \cdots = 1 + 1 + 1 + \cdots = \infty\). Yet no reasonable person would pay more than a few pounds to enter. Something is wrong with pure expected-value reasoning.

Daniel Bernoulli’s resolution (1738) was that people maximise the expected utility of outcomes, not their expected monetary value. If utility grows more slowly than wealth — as with \(U(W) = \ln W\) — the expected utility of the St Petersburg game is finite, matching observed willingness to pay.

Definition: Expected Utility

For a lottery with outcomes \(W_1, \ldots, W_n\) occurring with probabilities \(p_1, \ldots, p_n\) (where \(\sum p_i = 1\)), the expected utility is \(EU = \sum_{i=1}^{n} p_i \cdot U(W_i)\). An agent is an expected utility maximiser if they rank lotteries according to their EU.

Geometric Solution: EU vs U(EV)

The graph below shows a concave utility function \(U(W) = \sqrt{W}\). Two wealth outcomes — a bad state (low wealth) and a good state (high wealth) — are marked on the curve. The grey dashed chord connecting them shows all probability-weighted averages of utility. For a given probability \(p\) of the bad state, the expected utility EU falls on the chord at the expected wealth EV. By Jensen’s inequality (concavity), EU is always below \(U(EV)\) — the utility of the expected wealth. Adjust the sliders to see how the gap changes with the probability and the spread of outcomes.

Figure 17.1 — For a concave utility function, EU (point on the chord) lies below U(EV) (point on the curve). The gap reflects the utility cost of bearing risk.

Example: The Travel Insurance Decision

With wealth £80k and a 5% chance of losing £60k (cancellation), the expected wealth is £77k. Using \(U = \sqrt{W}\): \(EU = 0.05\sqrt{20} + 0.95\sqrt{80} \approx 8.72\). The utility of the expected wealth is \(\sqrt{77} \approx 8.77\). The gap \(8.77 - 8.72 = 0.05\) may seem small, but it implies a willingness to pay roughly £0.9k for insurance — even against a risk with expected loss of only £3k. The concavity of utility makes insurance valuable.

17.2 Risk Aversion

Intuition: Certainty Equivalent and Risk Premium

Two monetary measures quantify how much an agent dislikes risk. The certainty equivalent (CE) is the certain wealth level that yields the same utility as the risky gamble: \(U(CE) = EU\). A risk-averse agent has \(CE < EV\): they would accept a certain sum below the expected value in order to escape uncertainty.

The risk premium (RP) is the maximum amount the agent would pay to replace the gamble with its expected value: \(RP = EV - CE\). For a concave utility function, \(RP > 0\). For a linear utility function (risk-neutral agent), \(RP = 0\). For a convex utility function (risk-loving agent), \(RP < 0\) — the agent prefers the gamble and must be compensated to accept a certain outcome.

Definitions: CE and Risk Premium

The certainty equivalent is \(CE = U^{-1}(EU)\). The risk premium is \(RP = EV - CE\). A concave utility function implies \(CE < EV\) and \(RP > 0\) (risk aversion); a linear function implies \(CE = EV\) and \(RP = 0\) (risk neutrality); a convex function implies \(CE > EV\) and \(RP < 0\) (risk loving).

Geometric Solution: Reading CE and RP from the Graph

The graph shows CE and RP for three types of utility function. The EU level is marked as a horizontal line; where it intersects the utility curve gives CE on the horizontal axis. The gap between EV and CE is the risk premium. Toggle between utility types to see how the relationship reverses for a risk-loving agent.

Figure 17.2 — CE and risk premium for three utility types. Risk aversion: CE < EV, RP > 0. Risk neutrality: CE = EV, RP = 0. Risk loving: CE > EV, RP < 0.

Common Mistake: Confusing EU with EV

Expected value (EV) is a weighted average of monetary outcomes: \(EV = \sum p_i W_i\). Expected utility (EU) is a weighted average of utility values: \(EU = \sum p_i U(W_i)\). They are only equal when \(U\) is linear. The entire content of expected utility theory is that people care about EU, not EV, which is why they are willing to pay a risk premium to avoid uncertainty.

Example: Computing the Risk Premium

With \(U = \sqrt{W}\), \(W_B = 20\), \(W_G = 80\), and \(p = 0.4\): \(EU = 0.4\sqrt{20} + 0.6\sqrt{80} \approx 7.15\). The CE is \(EU^2 \approx 51.1\) and the EV is \(0.4 \times 20 + 0.6 \times 80 = 56\). The risk premium is \(56 - 51.1 = \pounds4.9\text{k}\). This agent would forego £4,900 in expected wealth for the security of a certain outcome.

17.3 Insurance

Intuition: Trading Across States of the World

The state-contingent framework reframes insurance geometrically. Instead of thinking about probabilities and payoffs, we think about consumption in two possible states of the world: the bad state (loss occurs) and the good state (no loss). The consumer’s uninsured endowment is a point in this two-dimensional space with high consumption in the good state and low consumption in the bad state.

Insurance allows the consumer to trade good-state consumption for bad-state consumption, moving along a budget line in state-contingent space. With actuarially fair insurance (premium equal to the expected payout), the budget line has slope \(-p/(1-p)\). For a risk-averse expected utility maximiser, the optimal choice is always full insurance — moving exactly to the 45° certainty line, where consumption is equal in both states.

Definition: Actuarially Fair Insurance

Insurance with coverage \(k\) is actuarially fair if the premium equals the expected payout: premium \(= p \cdot k\). The consumer’s budget line in state-contingent space has slope \(-p/(1-p)\) and passes through the endowment. A risk-averse EU maximiser always chooses full coverage (\(k = L\)) with fair insurance.

Geometric Solution: Moving to the Certainty Line

The graph shows state-contingent consumption space. The uninsured endowment lies below the 45° certainty line. Dragging the coverage slider moves the bundle along the fair-insurance budget line towards full certainty. The expected utility indifference curve (in purple) rises as coverage increases, and reaches its highest attainable level exactly on the 45° line.

Figure 17.3 — State-contingent consumption diagram. Fair insurance moves the bundle along the blue budget line toward the 45° certainty line. Full coverage is optimal for a risk-averse agent.

Key Insight: Why Full Insurance is Optimal

At any point on the 45° certainty line, the slope of the expected-utility indifference curve equals \(-p/(1-p)\) — exactly the slope of the fair-insurance budget line. Tangency therefore occurs on the certainty line itself. Any premium loading above the actuarially fair rate steepens the budget line, shifting the tangency below the 45° line and inducing partial insurance. This is why most real insurance is less than fully comprehensive: insurers charge loadings to cover administrative costs and profit margins.

17.4 Diversification

Intuition: Not All Eggs in One Basket

Insurance transfers idiosyncratic risk to an insurer who can pool it. But many financial risks cannot be insured directly — they must be managed by spreading wealth across assets whose returns do not move together. This is diversification. The key result is that the risk of a portfolio depends not just on the risks of the individual assets, but on how they are correlated. When two assets move in opposite directions, their risks partially cancel.

Consider a low-risk bond fund and a high-risk equity index. If their returns are perfectly correlated (\(\rho = 1\)), mixing them moves you along a straight line between the two assets in risk-return space — no diversification benefit. If correlation is imperfect (\(\rho < 1\)), the portfolio curve bows left, offering lower risk for the same expected return. When \(\rho = -1\), perfect hedging is possible: some combination of the two assets produces zero variance.

Definition: Portfolio Variance

For weight \(w\) in Asset A and \(1-w\) in Asset B, the portfolio expected return is \(\mu_p = w\mu_A + (1-w)\mu_B\) and variance is \(\sigma_p^2 = w^2\sigma_A^2 + (1-w)^2\sigma_B^2 + 2w(1-w)\rho\,\sigma_A\sigma_B\), where \(\rho \in [-1,1]\) is the correlation coefficient between the two asset returns.

Geometric Solution: The Risk-Return Frontier

The graph plots all achievable risk-return combinations for a portfolio of two assets. Move the correlation slider to see how the frontier changes shape. When \(\rho\) falls below 1, the frontier bows left and the minimum-variance portfolio (green dot) achieves lower risk than either asset alone. The highlighted blue segment is the efficient frontier — no rational investor should hold a portfolio below the minimum-variance point, since a higher expected return is available at the same risk.

Figure 17.4 — Portfolio risk-return frontier for two assets. Lower correlation bows the frontier leftward, reducing the minimum achievable variance. The blue segment is the efficient frontier.

Example: UK Equities and Gilts, 2000–2024

Over the past 25 years, UK equities (FTSE All-Share) and long-dated gilts have had a correlation close to zero — and during market stress episodes (the dot-com crash, the 2008 financial crisis) it turned negative. A 60/40 equity-gilt portfolio achieved a Sharpe ratio substantially above either asset held alone. The 2022 gilt crisis showed, however, that correlations are not stable: when inflation rose sharply, gilts and equities fell together, breaking the diversification benefit precisely when it was most needed.

17.5 Formal Derivations

Arrow-Pratt Measures of Risk Aversion

The degree of risk aversion is characterised by the curvature of the utility function. The Arrow-Pratt coefficient of absolute risk aversion is:

\[A(W) = -\frac{U''(W)}{U'(W)}\]

A higher value of \(A(W)\) means the agent is more risk averse. For \(U = \sqrt{W}\): \(U' = \frac{1}{2}W^{-1/2}\), \(U'' = -\frac{1}{4}W^{-3/2}\), so \(A(W) = \frac{1}{2W}\). Risk aversion decreases with wealth (decreasing absolute risk aversion, DARA), which is empirically realistic: wealthier people tend to hold more risky assets in absolute terms.

The coefficient of relative risk aversion scales by wealth:

\[R(W) = W \cdot A(W) = -\frac{W \cdot U''(W)}{U'(W)}\]

For \(U = \ln W\): \(U' = 1/W\), \(U'' = -1/W^2\), so \(R(W) = 1\) (constant relative risk aversion, CRRA). The general CRRA utility family is \(U(W) = W^{1-\gamma}/(1-\gamma)\) for \(\gamma \neq 1\), where \(\gamma\) is the coefficient of relative risk aversion. Values of \(\gamma\) between 1 and 4 are most consistent with observed asset-pricing data.

Optimal Insurance with an Actuarially Fair Premium

A consumer with initial wealth \(W\) faces loss \(L\) with probability \(p\). She purchases coverage \(k \in [0,L]\) at premium \(pk\) (actuarially fair). Expected utility as a function of coverage is:

\[EU(k) = p \cdot U\bigl(W - L + k(1-p)\bigr) + (1-p) \cdot U\bigl(W - pk\bigr)\]

Differentiating with respect to \(k\) and setting to zero:

\[\frac{dEU}{dk} = p(1-p)\,U'(C_B) - p(1-p)\,U'(C_G) = 0 \quad \Rightarrow \quad U'(C_B) = U'(C_G)\]

Since \(U'' < 0\) implies \(U'\) is strictly decreasing, the only solution is \(C_B = C_G\), i.e., full insurance (\(k = L\)). The consumer equates marginal utility across all states, which requires equal consumption in both states.

The Arrow Approximation for the Risk Premium

For small risks, the risk premium can be approximated using a second-order Taylor expansion. Consider adding a zero-mean noise term \(\tilde\varepsilon\) (variance \(\sigma^2\)) to certain wealth \(W\). The risk premium \(\pi\) satisfying \(U(W - \pi) = E[U(W + \tilde\varepsilon)]\) is approximately:

\[\pi \approx \tfrac{1}{2}\,A(W)\cdot\sigma^2\]

The risk premium is proportional to absolute risk aversion and to the variance of the risk. Doubling either one doubles the premium. This formula also shows why risk aversion, not just the sign of \(U''\), determines the cost of risk: two agents with the same \(U''\) but different wealth levels will pay different risk premiums if they have DARA preferences.

Portfolio Theory: Minimum-Variance Portfolio

Minimising \(\sigma_p^2 = w^2\sigma_A^2 + (1-w)^2\sigma_B^2 + 2w(1-w)\rho\,\sigma_A\sigma_B\) with respect to \(w\) gives the minimum-variance weight:

\[w^* = \frac{\sigma_B^2 - \rho\,\sigma_A\sigma_B}{\sigma_A^2 + \sigma_B^2 - 2\rho\,\sigma_A\sigma_B}\]

When \(\rho = -1\), the minimum variance is zero and the perfectly hedged portfolio has \(w^* = \sigma_B/(\sigma_A + \sigma_B)\). When \(\rho = 1\), the frontier degenerates to a straight line and the minimum variance equals \((\sigma_A\sigma_B/(\sigma_A+\sigma_B))^2\cdot(\sigma_A+\sigma_B)^2/\sigma_A^2\); no diversification benefit exists.

CAPM: Pricing Systematic Risk

In equilibrium, when all investors hold mean-variance efficient portfolios, only undiversifiable (systematic) risk is compensated. The Capital Asset Pricing Model states:

\[E[R_i] - R_f = \beta_i\bigl(E[R_m] - R_f\bigr), \qquad \beta_i = \frac{\text{Cov}(R_i, R_m)}{\text{Var}(R_m)}\]

where \(R_f\) is the risk-free rate and \(E[R_m]\) is the expected market return. Idiosyncratic risk can be diversified away and attracts no premium. A share with \(\beta > 1\) (e.g., technology stocks) is more volatile than the market and commands a higher expected return; one with \(\beta < 1\) (e.g., utilities) is defensive. The CAPM is the direct equilibrium consequence of the mean-variance preferences developed in this chapter.

Summary

Expected utility theory resolves the St Petersburg Paradox and explains a wide range of observed behaviour under uncertainty. By maximising the expected utility of wealth rather than expected wealth itself, agents with concave utility — risk-averse agents — assign extra weight to bad outcomes relative to their probability. The utility cost of risk is captured monetarily by the risk premium: the amount a risk-averse agent would forego in expected wealth to eliminate uncertainty entirely.

The state-contingent consumption diagram provides a clean geometric treatment of insurance. The uninsured endowment lies off the 45° certainty line; actuarially fair insurance is a budget line with slope \(-p/(1-p)\); and the tangency condition confirms that full coverage is optimal for any risk-averse expected utility maximiser. Premium loading above the actuarially fair rate shifts the optimal bundle below the certainty line, explaining why real-world insurance is rarely fully comprehensive.

Diversification addresses risk from the portfolio side. Combining assets with imperfect correlation reduces the variance of the portfolio below the weighted average of the individual variances. The risk-return frontier bows leftward as correlation falls, and the efficient frontier identifies the best achievable trade-off. In competitive equilibrium, the CAPM shows that only systematic (non-diversifiable) risk carries a return premium — idiosyncratic risk is unrewarded because it can be eliminated for free.
