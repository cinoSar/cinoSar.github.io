The Problem: The Logic of Strategic Interaction

During the Cold War, the United States and the Soviet Union each faced a stark choice: build more nuclear weapons or negotiate disarmament. If both disarmed, the world would be safer and both sides could redirect military spending to productive uses. But if one side disarmed whilst the other built weapons, the disarmer would be dangerously vulnerable. Each side’s best move depended entirely on what the other did.

This is not just a geopolitical curiosity — it is the logic of strategic interaction that pervades economics. Firms deciding whether to enter a market, workers negotiating wages, countries setting trade policy, and consumers bidding in auctions all face decisions where the outcome depends on others’ choices. Game theory provides the formal framework for analysing these situations.

Key Insight

Game theory studies situations where the payoff to each decision-maker depends on the actions of all participants. A game is defined by its players, their available strategies, and the payoffs for each combination of strategies. The central solution concept is Nash equilibrium: a set of strategies from which no player can profitably deviate unilaterally.

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to represent strategic interactions as normal-form and extensive-form games, identify dominant strategies and Nash equilibria in pure and mixed strategies, apply backward induction to sequential games, analyse repeated games and the conditions under which cooperation can be sustained, and apply game theory to entry deterrence, bargaining, and auction design.

14.1 Normal-Form Games

Intuition: Players, Strategies, Payoffs

A normal-form (or strategic-form) game is the simplest way to represent a strategic interaction. It specifies three elements: the players, the strategies available to each player, and the payoffs each player receives for every possible combination of strategies.

Definition: Dominant Strategy

A strategy is dominant if it yields a higher payoff than any alternative strategy, regardless of what the other players do. If both players have a dominant strategy, the game has a dominant strategy equilibrium — each player plays their dominant strategy, and neither has any incentive to deviate.

The most famous game in economics is the Prisoners’ Dilemma. Two suspects are held separately. Each can either Cooperate (stay silent) or Defect (confess). If both cooperate, each gets a light sentence. If both defect, each gets a moderate sentence. If one cooperates and the other defects, the defector goes free and the cooperator gets a heavy sentence. Defecting is a dominant strategy for both — yet mutual cooperation would make both better off. The tension between individual rationality and collective welfare is the heart of the dilemma.

Not Every Game Has a Dominant Strategy

The Prisoners’ Dilemma is special because both players have dominant strategies. In many games — Battle of the Sexes, Hawk–Dove, Matching Pennies — players’ best responses depend on what the other does. For these games, we need the more general concept of Nash equilibrium.

14.2 Nash Equilibrium

Intuition: Mutual Best Responses

A Nash equilibrium is a combination of strategies — one for each player — such that no player can improve their payoff by unilaterally changing their strategy. Each player is playing a best response to the other. It is not necessarily the best outcome for society, nor even for the players themselves (as the Prisoners’ Dilemma shows), but it is stable: once reached, no one has an incentive to move.

Definition: Mixed Strategy

A pure strategy is a definite choice of action. A mixed strategy is a probability distribution over pure strategies — the player randomises, choosing each action with a specified probability. Nash proved that every finite game has at least one Nash equilibrium, possibly in mixed strategies. In a mixed-strategy NE, each player is indifferent between the strategies they randomise over.

Finding a mixed-strategy NE requires setting each player’s expected payoffs equal across the strategies in the mix. If Player 1 mixes between strategies A and B, Player 2’s mixing probabilities must make Player 1 indifferent between A and B. This “indifference principle” is the key computational tool.

Key Insight

Games can have zero, one, or multiple pure-strategy Nash equilibria — but Nash’s theorem guarantees at least one NE exists when mixed strategies are allowed. The Prisoners’ Dilemma has one pure NE (Defect, Defect). Battle of the Sexes has two pure NE and one mixed NE. Matching Pennies has no pure NE but one mixed NE at (½, ½).

14.3 Sequential Games

Intuition: Thinking Backwards

In a sequential game (or extensive-form game), players move in order, and later movers can observe what earlier movers did. The natural solution method is backward induction: start at the end of the game tree, determine the optimal action at each final decision node, then work backwards to the beginning. The resulting strategy profile is a subgame perfect equilibrium (SPE) — it is a Nash equilibrium in every subgame.

Definition: Subgame Perfect Equilibrium

A subgame perfect equilibrium (SPE) is a strategy profile that constitutes a Nash equilibrium in every subgame of the original game. It rules out “incredible threats” — promises or threats that would not be in a player’s interest to carry out if the relevant subgame were actually reached.

The classic example is the entry game: an incumbent monopolist threatens to start a price war if an entrant enters the market. In the normal form, “enter, accommodate” and “stay out, fight” are both Nash equilibria. But backward induction reveals that “fight” is not credible: if the entrant does enter, the incumbent prefers to accommodate. The only SPE is “enter, accommodate.”

Example: Credible Commitment

If the incumbent could commit to fighting — for example, by building excess capacity that makes fighting less costly — the threat becomes credible. The entrant, foreseeing a fight, might choose to stay out. This is the logic of strategic commitment: taking an irreversible action that changes the payoffs of future decisions. We saw this in Chapter 13 with Stackelberg’s first-mover advantage.

14.4 Repeated Games

Intuition: The Shadow of the Future

In a one-shot Prisoners’ Dilemma, defection is the only NE. But most real interactions are repeated — firms compete quarter after quarter, countries negotiate year after year. When players expect to interact again, the shadow of the future changes the calculus: cooperating today can be sustained if the threat of future punishment (retaliation by the other player) outweighs the short-term gain from defecting.

Definition: Grim Trigger Strategy

Under a grim trigger strategy, a player cooperates as long as the other player has always cooperated. If the other player ever defects, the grim trigger player defects forever after. Cooperation is sustainable in an infinitely repeated game if the discount factor \(\delta\) is high enough: the present value of future cooperation must exceed the one-shot gain from defection.

The folk theorem extends this insight: in an infinitely repeated game with sufficiently patient players, any individually rational payoff can be sustained as a Nash equilibrium. This is both powerful and troubling — it means theory alone cannot pin down a unique prediction for repeated games.

Key Insight

Cooperation in a repeated Prisoners’ Dilemma requires \(\delta \geq \frac{T - R}{T - P}\), where \(T\) is the temptation payoff (defect whilst other cooperates), \(R\) is the reward (mutual cooperation), and \(P\) is the punishment (mutual defection). More patient players (higher δ) and smaller temptation gains make cooperation easier to sustain.

14.5 Applications

Entry Deterrence

We previewed entry deterrence in Section 14.3. A richer version has the incumbent choosing an investment level (capacity, advertising, R&D) before the entrant decides whether to enter. If the investment credibly reduces the entrant’s post-entry profit below zero, entry is deterred. The key is credibility: the investment must be sunk (irreversible) so the incumbent cannot walk it back after the entrant decides.

Example: Predatory Pricing

In the 1990s, several low-cost airlines accused established carriers of predatory pricing — temporarily cutting fares below cost to drive out entrants, then raising prices once the threat was gone. Game theory shows this strategy is only rational if the incumbent’s losses during the price war are less than the present value of monopoly profits after the entrant exits. The UK Competition and Markets Authority (CMA) treats predatory pricing as an abuse of dominant position.

Bargaining

Nash’s bargaining solution and Rubinstein’s alternating-offers model show how two parties divide a surplus. In Rubinstein’s model, players take turns proposing a split, and the other player accepts or rejects. If both are equally patient, the first mover gets slightly more than half (a first-mover advantage). As the time between offers shrinks, the outcome converges to the Nash bargaining solution: an even split if the parties are symmetric.

Auctions

Auctions are games where bidders compete for an item by submitting bids. The four standard auction formats — English (ascending), Dutch (descending), first-price sealed-bid, and second-price sealed-bid (Vickrey) — differ in their rules but share a common strategic structure. The revenue equivalence theorem states that under certain conditions (risk-neutral bidders, independent private values), all four formats yield the same expected revenue to the seller.

Definition: Vickrey Auction

In a Vickrey (second-price sealed-bid) auction, each bidder submits a sealed bid. The highest bidder wins but pays the second-highest bid. The dominant strategy is to bid one’s true valuation: there is no incentive to shade the bid down or to bid above one’s value. This incentive compatibility makes the Vickrey auction a cornerstone of mechanism design.

14.6 Formal Derivations

Mixed-Strategy Nash Equilibrium

Consider a 2×2 game with payoff matrix:

\[ \begin{pmatrix} (a_1, a_2) & (b_1, b_2) \\ (c_1, c_2) & (d_1, d_2) \end{pmatrix} \]

Let Player 1 play Top with probability \(p\) and Player 2 play Left with probability \(q\). Player 1’s expected payoffs are:

\[ E[\pi_1 | \text{Top}] = q \cdot a_1 + (1-q) \cdot b_1, \qquad E[\pi_1 | \text{Bottom}] = q \cdot c_1 + (1-q) \cdot d_1 \]

For Player 1 to mix, these must be equal:

\[ q \cdot a_1 + (1-q) \cdot b_1 = q \cdot c_1 + (1-q) \cdot d_1 \quad \Rightarrow \quad q^* = \frac{d_1 - b_1}{a_1 - b_1 - c_1 + d_1} \]

Similarly, for Player 2 to mix:

\[ p^* = \frac{d_2 - c_2}{a_2 - c_2 - b_2 + d_2} \]

Notice the counterintuitive result: Player 1’s mixing probability is determined by Player 2’s payoffs, and vice versa. Each player’s mix is calibrated to make the other player indifferent.

Subgame Perfect Equilibrium via Backward Induction

Consider the entry game: Entrant chooses Enter (E) or Stay Out (S). If E, Incumbent chooses Accommodate (A) or Fight (F). Payoffs: (E, A) gives (2, 1); (E, F) gives (−1, −1); S gives (0, 3).

Step 1 (last mover): If Entrant enters, Incumbent compares A (payoff 1) to F (payoff −1). Incumbent chooses A.

Step 2 (first mover): Entrant anticipates accommodation. Enter gives payoff 2, Stay Out gives 0. Entrant enters.

SPE: (Enter, Accommodate) with payoffs (2, 1). The “Stay Out, Fight” NE is eliminated because Fight is not credible.

Repeated Prisoners’ Dilemma

Standard PD payoffs: mutual cooperation gives \(R = 3\), mutual defection gives \(P = 1\), temptation gives \(T = 5\), sucker gives \(S = 0\). Under grim trigger with discount factor \(\delta\):

Cooperate forever: payoff = \(R + \delta R + \delta^2 R + \ldots = \frac{R}{1-\delta}\).

Defect once, then punished: payoff = \(T + \delta P + \delta^2 P + \ldots = T + \frac{\delta P}{1-\delta}\).

Cooperation is sustainable if:

\[ \frac{R}{1-\delta} \geq T + \frac{\delta P}{1-\delta} \quad \Rightarrow \quad \delta \geq \frac{T - R}{T - P} = \frac{5 - 3}{5 - 1} = \frac{1}{2} \]

So cooperation can be sustained whenever \(\delta \geq 0.5\). More patient players and less tempting deviations make cooperation easier.

Revenue Equivalence

With \(n\) risk-neutral bidders whose valuations are i.i.d. draws from a distribution \(F\) on \([0, \bar{v}]\), the expected revenue in any standard auction equals the expected value of the second-highest order statistic:

\[ E[\text{Revenue}] = E[v_{(n-1:n)}] = \int_0^{\bar{v}} v \cdot n(n-1) F(v)^{n-2} f(v) [1-F(v)] \, dv \]

This holds for English, Dutch, first-price sealed-bid, and Vickrey auctions. The result breaks down with risk-averse bidders, correlated values, or asymmetric bidders.

Summary

Game theory provides the mathematical language for analysing strategic interaction. Normal-form games represent simultaneous decisions with payoff matrices; extensive-form games represent sequential decisions with game trees. The central solution concept, Nash equilibrium, captures the idea of mutual best response — no player can profitably deviate alone. Nash’s theorem guarantees that at least one NE exists, though it may be in mixed strategies.

Sequential games add the refinement of subgame perfection, which eliminates Nash equilibria that rely on incredible threats. Backward induction is the practical tool for finding SPE. The entry game illustrates how commitment — making threats credible through irreversible investment — can change the equilibrium outcome.

Repeated games transform the analysis of cooperation. The folk theorem shows that patient players can sustain a wide range of outcomes, including mutual cooperation in the Prisoners’ Dilemma. The threshold discount factor provides a precise condition for when cooperation is viable. Applications to entry deterrence, bargaining, and auctions demonstrate the breadth and power of game-theoretic reasoning.
