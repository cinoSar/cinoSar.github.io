The Problem: Is the New Laboratory Worth It?

A university is considering investing £2 million in a new research laboratory. The facility would generate £200,000 per year in research income for 20 years. On the surface, the total income (£4 million) far exceeds the cost (£2 million). But this reasoning is flawed: it ignores the fact that a pound received in 20 years is worth less than a pound received today. If the university could earn 8% per year by investing the £2 million in financial markets instead, is the laboratory still worthwhile?

This is the fundamental question of intertemporal economics: how do we compare costs and benefits that occur at different points in time? The answer lies in the concept of present value — the value today of a sum to be received in the future, discounted at the appropriate interest rate. This chapter develops the tools for evaluating investments, modelling consumers’ saving and borrowing decisions, and understanding how interest rates are determined in capital markets.

Key Insight

A pound today is worth more than a pound tomorrow, because today’s pound can be invested to earn interest. The present value of a future payment \(X\) received in \(t\) years at interest rate \(r\) is \(PV = X/(1+r)^t\). An investment is worthwhile if and only if the net present value (NPV) — the present value of all benefits minus all costs — is positive.

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to compute the present value of any stream of future payments, apply the NPV rule and internal rate of return to evaluate investment projects, model intertemporal consumption choice with a two-period budget constraint, explain the inverse relationship between bond prices and interest rates, and derive the Fisher equation linking nominal and real interest rates.

16.1 Present Value

Intuition: Discounting the Future

If the interest rate is 5%, £100 invested today grows to £105 in one year. Turning this around, £105 received in one year is worth only £100 today — we discount the future payment by dividing by \((1 + r)\). Over \(t\) years, the present value of a single payment \(X\) is:

\[PV = \frac{X}{(1+r)^t}\]

For a stream of payments \(X_1, X_2, \ldots, X_T\) received at the end of each year, the present value is the sum of all discounted payments:

\[PV = \sum_{t=1}^{T} \frac{X_t}{(1+r)^t}\]

Definition: Net Present Value

The net present value (NPV) of an investment that costs \(C_0\) today and generates a stream of future cash flows is \(\text{NPV} = -C_0 + \sum_{t=1}^{T} X_t / (1+r)^t\). The NPV rule: invest if and only if NPV > 0.

Example: The University Laboratory

At r = 5%, the PV of 20 annual payments of £200,000 is £2,492,400 — the NPV is +£492,400, so the investment is worthwhile. At r = 10%, the PV falls to £1,702,700, giving NPV = −£297,300 — the investment destroys value. The interest rate is the crucial variable.

16.2 Investment Decisions

Intuition: NPV and IRR

The NPV rule is straightforward: accept a project if NPV > 0, reject if NPV < 0. But decision-makers often want a single number that summarises a project’s attractiveness without needing to specify a discount rate. The internal rate of return (IRR) fills this role: it is the discount rate that makes NPV exactly zero.

Definition: Internal Rate of Return

The internal rate of return (IRR) is the value of \(r\) that solves \(\text{NPV}(r) = 0\). The IRR rule: invest if IRR exceeds the cost of capital (the interest rate the firm must pay to borrow). For conventional projects (one initial outlay followed by positive cash flows), the IRR rule and NPV rule always agree.

Limitations of IRR

The IRR rule has pitfalls. For non-conventional projects (where cash flows change sign more than once), multiple IRRs can exist. For mutually exclusive projects (choose one or the other), the project with the higher IRR is not always the better investment — NPV should be used to compare. And IRR cannot handle changing discount rates. When in doubt, use NPV.

16.3 Intertemporal Choice

Intuition: Saving and Borrowing

Consumers face intertemporal choices every day: save now for retirement or spend today? Take out a mortgage or rent? The two-period model captures the essence of these decisions. A consumer receives income \(Y_1\) in period 1 and \(Y_2\) in period 2, and chooses consumption \(C_1\) and \(C_2\) to maximise utility subject to the intertemporal budget constraint:

\[C_1 + \frac{C_2}{1+r} = Y_1 + \frac{Y_2}{1+r}\]

The right-hand side is the consumer’s lifetime wealth in present-value terms. The consumer can shift consumption across periods by saving (if \(C_1 < Y_1\)) or borrowing (if \(C_1 > Y_1\)) at the interest rate \(r\).

Definition: Intertemporal Budget Constraint

The intertemporal budget constraint shows all combinations of \(C_1\) and \(C_2\) the consumer can afford. Its slope is \(-(1+r)\): giving up one unit of \(C_1\) yields \(1+r\) units of \(C_2\) (the return from saving). A rise in \(r\) rotates the constraint around the endowment point, making saving more attractive (substitution effect) but also making the consumer richer if they are a net saver (income effect).

Geometric Solution: Two-Period Consumption

Key Insight

A rise in the interest rate has an ambiguous effect on saving. The substitution effect always increases saving (future consumption is cheaper). The income effect depends on whether the consumer is a net saver (richer, so saves less) or net borrower (poorer, so borrows less — effectively saves more). For net savers, the effects work in opposite directions, and the net effect is empirically small.

16.4 Bond Pricing and the Yield Curve

Intuition: Why Bond Prices Move Inversely to Interest Rates

A bond is a promise to pay a stream of fixed coupons plus a face value at maturity. The price of a bond is simply the present value of these future payments, discounted at the market interest rate (the yield). When interest rates rise, the discount factor increases, and the present value of the bond’s fixed payments falls. When interest rates fall, bond prices rise. This inverse relationship is one of the most important facts in finance.

Definition: Bond Price

The price of a bond with annual coupon \(c\), face value \(F\), maturity \(T\), and yield \(y\) is:

\[P = \sum_{t=1}^{T} \frac{c}{(1+y)^t} + \frac{F}{(1+y)^T} = c \cdot \frac{1 - (1+y)^{-T}}{y} + \frac{F}{(1+y)^T}\]

When the coupon rate equals the yield, the bond trades at par (\(P = F\)). When the coupon rate exceeds the yield, it trades at a premium; when below, at a discount.

Geometric Solution: Bond Price vs Yield

Example: UK Gilt Losses in 2022

In 2022, the Bank of England raised the base rate from 0.25% to 3.5%. Long-dated UK government bonds (gilts) suffered severe price falls. A 30-year gilt lost roughly 40% of its value. Pension funds holding leveraged gilt positions faced margin calls, triggering the September 2022 gilt market crisis. The lesson: the inverse price–yield relationship, amplified by long duration, can create dramatic losses when rates rise sharply.

16.5 Formal Derivations

The Fisher Equation

The Fisher equation connects nominal and real interest rates. If the nominal rate is \(i\), the inflation rate is \(\pi\), and the real rate is \(r\):

\[1 + i = (1 + r)(1 + \pi) \quad \Rightarrow \quad r \approx i - \pi\]

The approximation holds when \(r\) and \(\pi\) are small. Investors care about the real rate because it determines the growth in purchasing power. The nominal rate adjusts to compensate for expected inflation.

Optimal Intertemporal Allocation

The consumer maximises \(U(C_1, C_2)\) subject to \(C_1 + C_2/(1+r) = W\), where \(W = Y_1 + Y_2/(1+r)\) is lifetime wealth. The Lagrangian is:

\[ \mathcal{L} = U(C_1, C_2) - \lambda\left[C_1 + \frac{C_2}{1+r} - W\right] \]

First-order conditions:

\[ \frac{\partial U}{\partial C_1} = \lambda, \qquad \frac{\partial U}{\partial C_2} = \frac{\lambda}{1+r} \] \[ \Rightarrow \quad \frac{MU_1}{MU_2} = 1 + r \quad \Rightarrow \quad MRS_{C_1, C_2} = 1 + r \]

The consumer equates the marginal rate of substitution between present and future consumption to the gross interest rate. With Cobb-Douglas preferences \(U = C_1^{\alpha} C_2^{1-\alpha}\), the optimal choices are \(C_1^* = \alpha W\) and \(C_2^* = (1-\alpha)(1+r)W\).

Annuity Formula

The present value of an annuity paying \(X\) per year for \(T\) years at rate \(r\) is:

\[ PV = X \cdot \frac{1 - (1+r)^{-T}}{r} \]

As \(T \to \infty\), this converges to the perpetuity formula \(PV = X/r\). The university laboratory generating £200,000 per year forever at 5% would be worth £4 million.

Bond Duration

Macaulay duration is the weighted average time to receipt of a bond’s cash flows, where the weights are the present values of each cash flow as a fraction of the bond’s price:

\[ D = \frac{1}{P} \sum_{t=1}^{T} t \cdot \frac{CF_t}{(1+y)^t} \]

Modified duration \(D^* = D/(1+y)\) approximates the percentage price change for a small change in yield: \(\Delta P / P \approx -D^* \cdot \Delta y\). Longer-maturity bonds have greater duration and are more sensitive to rate changes.

IRR Computation

For the laboratory project with cost \(C_0 = 2000\) and annual cash flow \(X = 200\) for \(T = 20\) years, the IRR solves:

\[ 200 \cdot \frac{1 - (1+r)^{-20}}{r} = 2000 \quad \Rightarrow \quad \frac{1 - (1+r)^{-20}}{r} = 10 \]

This equation has no closed-form solution but can be solved numerically. The IRR is approximately 7.75%: the project is worthwhile if the cost of capital is below 7.75%.

Summary

The time value of money is the foundation of all intertemporal economics. A pound today is worth more than a pound tomorrow because it can be invested at the interest rate. Present value translates future cash flows into today’s pounds, enabling meaningful comparison of costs and benefits that occur at different times. The NPV rule — invest when NPV is positive — is the gold standard for project evaluation.

Consumers face intertemporal trade-offs too. The two-period model shows how saving and borrowing depend on the interest rate, income profile, and preferences for present versus future consumption. The substitution and income effects of an interest rate change work in opposite directions for savers, making the net effect on saving an empirical question.

Bond pricing illustrates present value in action. A bond’s price is the discounted value of its coupon stream and face value; it moves inversely to the yield. Duration measures a bond’s sensitivity to rate changes and explains why long-dated bonds are riskier in a rising-rate environment — a lesson the UK gilt market learned painfully in 2022.
