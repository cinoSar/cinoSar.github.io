The Problem: The Price of Phoning Home

Before 1984, British Telecom was the United Kingdom’s sole telephone provider. A three-minute daytime call cost roughly 25p — equivalent to well over £1 today. Business customers had no alternative, residential subscribers waited weeks for installation, and innovation was glacial. When the government privatised BT and allowed competition from Mercury Communications, call prices fell sharply and service improved. The difference was stark: a monopolist, facing no competitive pressure, had chosen high prices and low investment because it could.

In previous chapters, we studied firms that take the market price as given. A monopolist is the polar opposite: it is the market. Because it is the sole seller, its output decision determines the market price. This market power changes everything — the pricing rule, the welfare consequences, and the case for government intervention.

Key Insight

A monopolist faces a downward-sloping demand curve and must lower its price to sell more. This means marginal revenue is less than price: each extra unit sold brings in revenue on that unit but reduces revenue on all previous units. The monopolist produces where MR = MC and charges a price above marginal cost, creating a deadweight loss — units that are worth more to consumers than they cost to produce, but that the monopolist refuses to supply.

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to derive the monopolist’s profit-maximising price and quantity, compute the deadweight loss from monopoly relative to perfect competition, explain the Lerner index and its connection to demand elasticity, analyse natural monopoly and evaluate alternative regulatory approaches, and determine how a multi-plant monopolist allocates production across facilities.

11.1 Sources of Monopoly

A firm is a monopolist when it is the sole supplier of a good with no close substitutes. But why would an entire market have only one seller? Several mechanisms can create and sustain monopoly power.

Definition: Barriers to Entry

Barriers to entry are factors that prevent potential competitors from entering a market. Without barriers, the prospect of monopoly profits would attract entrants until profits were competed away (as in the long-run competitive model of Chapter 8). Persistent monopoly requires persistent barriers.

Legal barriers are the most straightforward. A patent grants an inventor the exclusive right to sell a product for a fixed period — typically 20 years. Pharmaceutical companies rely on patents to recoup research costs: a new drug can cost over £1 billion to develop, and without patent protection, generic competitors would copy it immediately. Government franchises and licences also create legal monopolies: until recently, Royal Mail held a statutory monopoly on letter delivery in the UK.

Natural monopoly arises when a single firm can supply the entire market at lower cost than two or more firms could. This typically happens in industries with very large fixed costs and low marginal costs — electricity transmission, water supply, railway infrastructure. The average cost curve declines over the entire relevant range of output, so a single firm is the most efficient market structure. We analyse this case in detail in Section 11.4.

Control of essential inputs can also sustain monopoly. De Beers historically controlled the vast majority of the world’s diamond supply. If a key raw material or technology is controlled by one firm, competitors simply cannot produce.

Network effects arise when the value of a product increases with the number of users. Social media platforms and operating systems exhibit strong network effects: everyone uses the same platform because everyone else is already there. This can create a “winner-takes-all” dynamic that is difficult for new entrants to break.

Example: Thames Water

Thames Water supplies water and sewerage services to 15 million customers in London and the Thames Valley. It would make no economic sense to build a second network of pipes and treatment works alongside the existing one. The enormous sunk costs of infrastructure create a natural monopoly, which is why the industry is regulated by Ofwat, the water services regulator.

11.2 Monopoly Pricing

Intuition: Why MR < P

A competitive firm can sell as much as it likes at the market price, so its marginal revenue equals price. A monopolist, by contrast, faces the entire market demand curve. To sell one more unit, it must lower the price — not just on the marginal unit, but on all the units it was already selling. This makes marginal revenue strictly less than price for every unit after the first.

Consider a simple linear demand curve \(P = a - bQ\). Total revenue is \(TR = PQ = aQ - bQ^2\), so marginal revenue is \(MR = a - 2bQ\). The MR curve has the same intercept as the demand curve but twice the slope — it lies below demand everywhere. The monopolist maximises profit by producing where MR = MC and then charging the price consumers are willing to pay for that quantity (read off the demand curve).

Key Insight

For a linear demand curve \(P = a - bQ\), the MR curve is \(MR = a - 2bQ\). It has the same vertical intercept and exactly twice the slope. The monopoly quantity is found where MR = MC, and the monopoly price is the demand curve evaluated at that quantity: \(P_m = a - bQ_m\). The gap \(P_m - MC\) is the monopolist’s markup.

As marginal cost rises, the monopolist reduces output, raises price, and earns a smaller profit. But even at relatively high costs, the monopolist still charges a price well above MC — the markup is a defining feature of monopoly. Only at MC = 50 (equal to the demand intercept) would the monopolist shut down entirely.

Common Misconception

The monopolist does not charge the highest possible price. It could charge a very high price, but then it would sell very few units. The profit-maximising price balances the higher margin per unit against the loss in quantity sold. Monopoly pricing is strategic, not arbitrary.

11.3 Deadweight Loss of Monopoly

Intuition: The Welfare Cost of Market Power

In Chapter 9, we measured welfare using consumer surplus (CS) and producer surplus (PS). Under perfect competition, price equals marginal cost, and the sum CS + PS is maximised. A monopolist restricts output below the competitive level and charges a price above MC. Some surplus that consumers would have enjoyed is transferred to the monopolist (higher PS), but some surplus is destroyed entirely — this is the deadweight loss (DWL).

Definition: Deadweight Loss of Monopoly

The deadweight loss is the reduction in total surplus (CS + PS) caused by the monopolist’s output restriction. It equals the area of the triangle between the demand curve and the MC curve, from the monopoly quantity \(Q_m\) to the competitive quantity \(Q_c\). These are units worth more to consumers than they cost to produce, but the monopolist withholds them to keep the price high.

Geometric Solution: Monopoly vs Competition

Use the toggle to switch between the competitive outcome and the monopoly outcome. Under competition, the firm produces where P = MC, and all surplus goes to CS + PS with no DWL. Under monopoly, output contracts, price rises, and the grey triangle shows the deadweight loss.

Figure 11.2 — Welfare under competition (left) and monopoly (right). The grey triangle is the deadweight loss from monopoly pricing.

The transfer from consumers to the monopolist (the rectangle between \(P_m\) and MC, from 0 to \(Q_m\)) is not a welfare loss to society as a whole — it is merely a redistribution from buyers to the seller. The true social cost is the DWL triangle: surplus that vanishes because the transactions between \(Q_m\) and \(Q_c\) never occur.

Example: Measuring the Cost

With demand \(P = 50 - Q\) and MC = £10, the competitive outcome is \(Q_c = 40, P_c = 10\). The monopoly outcome is \(Q_m = 20, P_m = 30\). The DWL is \(\frac{1}{2}(P_m - MC)(Q_c - Q_m) = \frac{1}{2}(20)(20) = 200\). This is surplus that neither consumers nor the monopolist captures — it is simply lost.

11.4 Natural Monopoly and Regulation

Intuition: When One Firm Is Cheapest

A natural monopoly exists when the average cost curve is still declining at the quantity the market demands. This happens when fixed costs are very large relative to marginal costs — think of the infrastructure required for water supply, electricity transmission, or railway tracks. Duplicating this infrastructure would be wasteful, so having a single supplier is efficient from a production standpoint.

The problem is that an unregulated natural monopolist would still set MR = MC and charge a price well above average cost. Regulators face a dilemma: forcing the firm to price at MC (the efficient outcome) may cause losses if MC < AC, because the firm cannot cover its fixed costs. The three main regulatory approaches each involve trade-offs.

Three Regulatory Approaches

Marginal-cost pricing (\(P = MC\)): maximises welfare but may require a government subsidy because the firm incurs losses when MC < AC. Average-cost pricing (\(P = AC\)): the firm breaks even without a subsidy, but some DWL remains because P > MC. Two-part tariff: a fixed access fee covers fixed costs, and a per-unit price equals MC. This achieves efficiency but may exclude consumers who cannot afford the access fee.

Geometric Solution: Regulatory Options

Adjust the fixed cost slider to see how the regulatory challenge changes with the size of fixed costs.

Figure 11.3 — Natural monopoly regulation. Compare unregulated monopoly with MC pricing, AC pricing, and two-part tariff outcomes.

Notice that as fixed costs rise, the gap between AC and MC widens, making the regulatory dilemma more acute. MC pricing generates larger losses that require bigger subsidies. AC pricing moves the price further from the efficient level, increasing DWL. The two-part tariff resolves the dilemma in principle but requires the regulator to know the firm’s true costs — information the firm has every incentive to misrepresent.

The Information Problem

All regulatory approaches require the regulator to know the firm’s cost structure. In practice, regulated firms typically know their own costs better than the regulator does, creating an information asymmetry. Firms may exaggerate costs to justify higher regulated prices — a problem we revisit in Chapter 19 on asymmetric information.

11.5 Multi-Plant Monopoly

Intuition: Allocating Production Across Plants

Many monopolists operate multiple production facilities. A brewery might have plants in London and Manchester, each with different cost structures. How should the monopolist allocate total output across plants to minimise cost?

The answer follows the equimarginal principle: allocate production so that marginal cost is equalised across plants. If one plant has lower MC at the margin, shift production towards it until the MCs are equal. The firm’s overall MC curve is the horizontal sum of the individual plants’ MC curves — at each MC level, add up the quantities each plant would produce.

Key Insight

A multi-plant monopolist minimises cost by equating marginal cost across all plants: \(MC_1(q_1) = MC_2(q_2) = \ldots = MC\). The firm’s aggregate MC is the horizontal sum of individual plant MCs. The monopolist then sets overall MR = aggregate MC to find total output and price.

Geometric Solution: Two-Plant Allocation

The graph shows a monopolist with two plants. Plant 1 has MC\(_1 = c_1 q_1\) and Plant 2 has MC\(_2 = c_2 q_2\). The right panel shows the aggregate MC (horizontal sum) and the MR curve. Adjust the cost parameters to see how production shifts between plants.

Figure 11.4 — Multi-plant monopoly. The firm equates MC across plants and sets MR equal to aggregate MC.

When the two plants have equal costs, production splits evenly. When one plant is cheaper, it takes a larger share. The aggregate MC curve is always flatter than either individual MC curve, because the firm has more flexibility to spread production. This means a multi-plant monopolist produces more (and charges less) than a single-plant monopolist with the same total capacity — diversification of production is beneficial even for a monopolist.

11.6 Formal Derivations

Monopoly Profit Maximisation

Let inverse demand be \(P(Q) = a - bQ\) and let the monopolist have constant marginal cost \(c\). The monopolist maximises profit:

\[ \pi = P(Q) \cdot Q - cQ = (a - bQ)Q - cQ = aQ - bQ^2 - cQ \]

The first-order condition is:

\[ \frac{d\pi}{dQ} = a - 2bQ - c = 0 \quad \Rightarrow \quad Q_m = \frac{a - c}{2b} \]

The monopoly price is:

\[ P_m = a - bQ_m = a - \frac{a - c}{2} = \frac{a + c}{2} \]

Monopoly profit is:

\[ \pi_m = (P_m - c)Q_m = \frac{(a - c)^2}{4b} \]

The second-order condition \(d^2\pi/dQ^2 = -2b < 0\) confirms this is a maximum.

The Lerner Index

The Lerner index measures market power as the proportional markup of price over marginal cost:

\[ L = \frac{P - MC}{P} \]

Under perfect competition, \(P = MC\) so \(L = 0\). For a monopolist, \(L > 0\). We can connect it to the price elasticity of demand. Since \(MR = P(1 + 1/\varepsilon_d)\) where \(\varepsilon_d < 0\), and the monopolist sets \(MR = MC\):

\[ P\left(1 + \frac{1}{\varepsilon_d}\right) = MC \quad \Rightarrow \quad \frac{P - MC}{P} = -\frac{1}{\varepsilon_d} \]

This is the inverse elasticity rule:

\[ L = \frac{P - MC}{P} = \frac{1}{|\varepsilon_d|} \]

A monopolist facing more elastic demand (higher \(|\varepsilon_d|\)) charges a smaller markup. A monopolist facing inelastic demand (low \(|\varepsilon_d|\)) charges a large markup. Note that a profit-maximising monopolist always operates on the elastic portion of the demand curve (\(|\varepsilon_d| > 1\)), because MR is negative in the inelastic region.

Deadweight Loss Formula

With linear demand \(P = a - bQ\) and constant MC = c, the competitive outcome is \(Q_c = (a - c)/b\), \(P_c = c\). The monopoly outcome is \(Q_m = (a - c)/(2b)\), \(P_m = (a + c)/2\). The DWL triangle has base \(Q_c - Q_m = (a - c)/(2b)\) and height \(P_m - c = (a - c)/2\):

\[ \text{DWL} = \frac{1}{2} \cdot \frac{a - c}{2b} \cdot \frac{a - c}{2} = \frac{(a - c)^2}{8b} \]

Comparing with monopoly profit \(\pi_m = (a - c)^2 / (4b)\), we see that \(\text{DWL} = \pi_m / 2\). The deadweight loss is exactly half the monopoly profit for this linear case.

Multi-Plant Monopolist

Suppose the firm has two plants with cost functions \(C_1(q_1) = \frac{1}{2}c_1 q_1^2\) and \(C_2(q_2) = \frac{1}{2}c_2 q_2^2\). Total output \(Q = q_1 + q_2\). The firm minimises cost for a given \(Q\) by equating marginal costs:

\[ MC_1 = c_1 q_1 = c_2 q_2 = MC_2 \quad \Rightarrow \quad q_1 = \frac{c_2}{c_1 + c_2} Q, \quad q_2 = \frac{c_1}{c_1 + c_2} Q \]

The aggregate MC is:

\[ MC(Q) = \frac{c_1 c_2}{c_1 + c_2} Q \]

The monopolist then sets \(MR = MC(Q)\) to find optimal total output, and allocates it across plants using the shares above.

Summary

Monopoly is the polar opposite of perfect competition. A monopolist faces the entire market demand curve, which means marginal revenue is less than price. The profit-maximising rule MR = MC leads to a quantity below the competitive level and a price above marginal cost. The gap between price and MC — measured by the Lerner index — is inversely related to the elasticity of demand.

The welfare consequences are clear: monopoly creates a deadweight loss by withholding output that would be worth more to consumers than it costs to produce. For a linear demand curve with constant MC, the DWL equals exactly half the monopoly profit — a useful benchmark for gauging the social cost of market power.

Natural monopoly introduces a regulatory dilemma. When average cost is declining over the relevant range, a single firm is the efficient producer, but an unregulated monopolist will exploit its position. Marginal-cost pricing achieves efficiency but may require subsidies; average-cost pricing avoids losses but leaves some DWL; two-part tariffs can achieve the best of both worlds but require good information and may exclude some consumers.
