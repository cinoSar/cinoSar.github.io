The Problem: Who Pays for London’s Pollution?

In 2019, London introduced the Ultra Low Emission Zone (ULEZ), charging the most polluting vehicles £12.50 per day to enter a zone covering most of inner London. By 2023 the zone had expanded to cover all 32 London boroughs. The aim was simple: make drivers bear the full cost of the pollution their vehicles create — not just the cost of petrol and wear and tear, but the damage inflicted on the lungs of the 9 million people who live, work, and breathe in the capital.

Why is a charge necessary at all? If driving creates costs for others, why don’t markets fix this automatically? The answer is that pollution is an externality: a cost (or benefit) that falls on parties outside the market transaction. The driver pays for fuel; the NHS pays to treat asthma patients. The market price of a journey captures only the private cost, not the social cost. This wedge between private and social incentives is the fundamental source of market failure in the presence of externalities.

A related failure arises with public goods: goods that are non-rival (one person’s consumption does not reduce availability to others) and non-excludable (it is impossible or prohibitively costly to prevent non-payers from benefiting). Clean air in London is both — reducing one person’s exposure to particulates does not reduce another’s, and the air cannot be fenced off to paying subscribers. Markets systematically underprovide such goods, because private providers cannot recover their costs from free-riders.

Key Insight

Markets are efficient only when the prices faced by buyers and sellers reflect all costs and benefits. Negative externalities (like pollution) cause overproduction because producers ignore external costs; positive externalities (like vaccinations) cause underproduction because producers ignore external benefits. Corrective policy — Pigovian taxes and subsidies, regulation, or Coasian bargaining — can restore efficiency by aligning private and social incentives.

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to identify negative and positive externalities and show their welfare effects using supply and demand diagrams, derive the optimal Pigovian tax or subsidy, explain the Coase theorem and its limitations, define public goods and explain the free-rider problem, use vertical summation to find the socially optimal provision of a public good, and state the Samuelson condition for public goods.

18.1 Externalities

Intuition: Private vs Social Cost

A steel mill that dumps waste into a river imposes costs on downstream fishermen, swimmers, and water companies that it does not pay for. These are external costs: costs borne by third parties who are not party to the market transaction. The firm’s private marginal cost (PMC) — the cost of producing one more tonne of steel — does not include this damage. The social marginal cost (SMC) is the sum of private cost and the marginal external cost (MEC):

\[\text{SMC} = \text{PMC} + \text{MEC}\]

Competitive markets equate price to PMC, not SMC. The market-clearing quantity \(Q_M\) satisfies \(P = \text{PMC}\), which exceeds the socially optimal quantity \(Q^*\) where \(P = \text{SMC}\). The extra units \(Q_M - Q^*\) cost society more (in SMC) than consumers value them (in demand price), generating a deadweight loss.

Definitions: Externality Types

A negative externality imposes costs on third parties (e.g., pollution, noise, congestion): SMC > PMC, markets overproduce. A positive externality confers benefits on third parties (e.g., vaccinations, education, R&D spillovers): social marginal benefit (SMB) > private marginal benefit (PMB), markets underproduce.

Example: Carbon Emissions and the ETS

The UK Emissions Trading Scheme (ETS) puts a price on carbon dioxide emissions from large industrial installations and power stations. When firms emit CO⊂2;, the social cost includes not only production costs but the present value of future climate damage — estimated by the UK government at around £248 per tonne of CO⊂2; in 2023 (the “social cost of carbon”). Without a carbon price, firms treat this external cost as zero, leading to overemission. The ETS forces them to hold allowances, converting the external cost into a private cost.

18.2 Solutions: Pigovian Taxes and Subsidies

Intuition: Internalising the Externality

The economist Arthur Pigou argued in 1920 that the correct policy response to a negative externality is a per-unit tax equal to the marginal external cost at the social optimum. A Pigovian tax of \(t^* = \text{MEC}(Q^*)\) raises the firm’s effective marginal cost from PMC to PMC + \(t\), shifting the supply curve up. When set correctly, the new market equilibrium coincides with the social optimum: \(Q_M(t^*) = Q^*\).

For positive externalities, the mirror image applies: a Pigovian subsidy equal to the marginal external benefit at the optimum lowers the private cost, stimulating production to the socially efficient level. The UK government’s subsidy for electric vehicle purchases and home insulation are examples of Pigovian-style subsidies targeting positive externalities (reduced emissions, energy efficiency spillovers).

Definition: Pigovian Tax

The Pigovian tax is a per-unit tax on production set equal to the marginal external cost at the socially optimal output: \(t^* = \text{MEC}(Q^*)\). It converts a negative externality into a private cost, aligning the firm’s supply curve with the social marginal cost curve.

The Information Problem

Setting a Pigovian tax correctly requires knowing MEC at the social optimum — precisely the information that is hardest to obtain. Overestimating MEC causes under-production; underestimating causes over-production. In practice, regulators use a range of instruments — quantity-based standards, cap-and-trade schemes, and liability rules — alongside taxes, partly because information about external costs is uncertain and contested.

18.3 The Coase Theorem

Intuition: Bargaining Without Government

In 1960, Ronald Coase proposed a radical alternative to Pigovian intervention. Suppose a factory and a neighbouring farmer can negotiate directly. If the factory has the right to pollute, the farmer will pay it to reduce emissions — up to the point where the marginal damage equals the marginal benefit to the factory of polluting. If the farmer has the right to clean air, the factory will pay the farmer to allow some pollution — up to the same point. In either case, the parties bargain to the same efficient level of the externality.

The Coase theorem states that if property rights are well-defined and transaction costs are zero, parties will bargain to the efficient outcome regardless of how property rights are initially assigned. The assignment of rights affects the distribution of income (who pays whom) but not the efficient quantity.

The Coase Theorem

When property rights are well defined and transaction costs are negligible, private bargaining between affected parties will produce the socially efficient outcome regardless of the initial assignment of property rights. Rights assignment affects distribution, not efficiency.

Geometric Solution: Two Roads to the Same Outcome

Why the Coase Theorem Often Fails in Practice

The theorem’s conclusion requires zero transaction costs — a heroic assumption. Identifying all affected parties (millions of drivers and asthma sufferers in London) and organising them to bargain is prohibitively costly. Asymmetric information about damage and benefit complicates negotiation. Strategic hold-out and free-riding within the victim group may prevent an efficient agreement even if one exists. Where transaction costs are high — the typical case for environmental externalities — Pigovian taxes, regulation, or tradeable permits remain the practical tools.

18.4 Public Goods

Intuition: Non-Rivalry and Non-Excludability

A good is non-rival if one person’s consumption does not reduce the amount available to others: a lighthouse beam guides all ships simultaneously without diminishing. A good is non-excludable if it is impossible or prohibitively costly to prevent non-payers from consuming it: once a firework display is lit, everyone in the vicinity can watch for free. A good that is both non-rival and non-excludable is a pure public good.

Non-excludability creates the free-rider problem: individuals have an incentive to withhold payment, hoping others will fund the good and they can consume it for free. If everyone free-rides, no one contributes, and the good is not provided — even though its total social value greatly exceeds its cost. This is why public goods are generally underprovided or not provided at all by private markets, requiring government provision or some alternative mechanism.

Definition: Pure Public Good

A pure public good is both non-rival (consumption by one does not reduce availability to others) and non-excludable (cannot be withheld from non-payers). Examples: national defence, basic research, broadcast radio, street lighting, clean air. Most real goods are impure: either partially rival (congestion) or excludable at a cost (toll roads).

Geometric Solution: Vertical Summation

For a private good, the market demand curve is found by horizontal summation of individual demands: at each price, add up quantities demanded across consumers. For a public good, the social marginal benefit is found by vertical summation: at each quantity, add up the marginal valuations of all consumers (because everyone benefits simultaneously from each unit). The Samuelson condition for social optimum is that the sum of marginal valuations equals marginal cost.

The graph shows two consumers’ demand curves for a public good, their vertical sum, and the marginal cost. The socially optimal quantity is where the vertical sum equals MC. The market provision (highest individual demand = MC) falls far short of the optimum because neither agent accounts for the other’s benefit. Example: UK Research Councils

Basic scientific research is a public good: its findings are non-rival (reading a paper does not prevent others reading it) and largely non-excludable (results are published and freely accessible). The private return to basic research is much lower than the social return because firms cannot appropriate the spillover benefits to other researchers, industries, and future generations. The UK Research and Innovation (UKRI) budget of around £8.5 billion per year (2023–24) exists precisely to close this gap — funding research that markets would underprovide.

18.5 Formal Derivations

The Optimal Pigovian Tax

Consider a competitive market with inverse demand \(P = D(Q)\) and private marginal cost \(\text{PMC}(Q)\). There is a constant marginal external cost MEC (the analysis extends straightforwardly to variable MEC). The social planner maximises total surplus net of external costs:

\[\max_Q \int_0^Q D(q)\,dq - \int_0^Q \text{PMC}(q)\,dq - \text{MEC} \cdot Q\]

The first-order condition is \(D(Q) = \text{PMC}(Q) + \text{MEC}\), i.e., \(P = \text{SMC}\). A per-unit tax \(t\) shifts the effective PMC to PMC + \(t\). Setting \(t^* = \text{MEC}\) achieves the social optimum. The Pigovian tax is therefore equal to MEC evaluated at the social optimum \(Q^*\). With non-constant MEC, the tax must be set equal to MEC(\(Q^*\)), not MEC(\(Q_M\)).

Deadweight Loss Formula

For linear demand \(D(Q) = a - bQ\) and linear PMC \(\text{PMC}(Q) = c + dQ\), the market equilibrium satisfies \(Q_M = (a-c)/(b+d)\). The social optimum satisfies \(Q^* = (a - c - \text{MEC})/(b+d)\). The deadweight loss is the area of the triangle between SMC and demand from \(Q^*\) to \(Q_M\):

\[\text{DWL} = \frac{1}{2} \cdot \text{MEC} \cdot (Q_M - Q^*) = \frac{\text{MEC}^2}{2(b+d)}\]

DWL is proportional to the square of the external cost, which is why large externalities are disproportionately damaging — and why corrective taxes for carbon emissions are so urgently advocated by economists.

The Samuelson Condition for Public Goods

Consider a two-consumer economy with a private good \(x\) and a public good \(G\). Consumer \(i\) has utility \(U_i(x_i, G)\) where \(G\) is the common quantity of the public good (non-rivalry). The economy’s resource constraint is \(x_1 + x_2 + c(G) = Y\), where \(c(G)\) is the cost of producing \(G\). The social planner maximises \(U_1\) subject to \(U_2 \geq \bar{U}_2\) and the resource constraint. The Lagrangian gives the first-order conditions:

\[\frac{\partial U_1/\partial G}{\partial U_1/\partial x_1} + \frac{\partial U_2/\partial G}{\partial U_2/\partial x_2} = c'(G)\]

The left-hand side is the sum of the individual marginal rates of substitution between the public good and the private good (individual marginal valuations). The right-hand side is the marginal cost of the public good. The Samuelson condition states:

\[\sum_{i=1}^{n} MRS_i^{G,x} = MC_G \quad \Leftrightarrow \quad \sum_{i=1}^{n} MV_i = MC_G\]

This is the vertical summation condition: the social optimum equates the sum of individual marginal valuations to marginal cost. It differs fundamentally from the private good optimum, where each individual’s marginal valuation equals MC (horizontal summation).

Lindahl Equilibrium

Erik Lindahl proposed a decentralised mechanism for public good provision. Each consumer \(i\) faces a personalised “Lindahl price” \(p_i^G\), representing their share of the public good’s cost. Consumer \(i\) demands the quantity \(G_i^*\) satisfying \(MV_i(G) = p_i^G\). The Lindahl equilibrium requires (i) markets clear: all consumers demand the same \(G^*\), and (ii) prices sum to marginal cost: \(\sum_i p_i^G = MC_G\).

A Lindahl equilibrium is Pareto optimal (it satisfies the Samuelson condition), but it is not implementable in practice because each consumer has an incentive to understate their marginal valuation in order to pay a lower Lindahl price — a revelation problem. This is the deep reason why public goods require non-market mechanisms for provision.

Summary

Externalities arise whenever a market transaction imposes costs or confers benefits on parties outside the transaction. Negative externalities cause markets to overproduce relative to the social optimum because producers ignore the external cost their output imposes on others. The resulting deadweight loss is the welfare cost of this misalignment, and it grows with the square of the marginal external cost. A Pigovian tax equal to the MEC at the social optimum eliminates this loss by making producers face the full social cost of their decisions.

The Coase theorem offers a complementary perspective: in a world of zero transaction costs and well-defined property rights, private bargaining achieves the efficient outcome without government intervention. The assignment of rights affects distribution, not efficiency. In practice, transaction costs are rarely negligible — the sheer number of affected parties, asymmetric information, and free-riding within victim groups make the conditions for Coasian bargaining hard to meet, particularly for diffuse environmental externalities like air quality or climate change.

Public goods present a different market failure. Non-rivalry means that providing a public good to one person costs no more than providing it to none, so the marginal cost of an additional user is zero. Non-excludability means that private providers cannot prevent free-riding and therefore cannot recover their costs through voluntary payments. The result is chronic underprovision. The Samuelson condition — the sum of marginal valuations equals marginal cost — identifies the socially optimal level, achieved through vertical summation of individual demand curves rather than the horizontal summation used for private goods.
