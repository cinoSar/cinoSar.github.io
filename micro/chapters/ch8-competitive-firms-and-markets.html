The Problem: Why Can't Dairy Farmers Set Their Own Price?

UK dairy farmers routinely complain that supermarkets pay them barely enough to cover costs — sometimes less. In 2015, the farm-gate price of milk fell below 24p per litre while production costs averaged 30p. Yet no individual farmer could raise their price: buyers would simply purchase from another farm. Each farmer is so small relative to the market that they have no influence over the going price.

This is the defining feature of perfect competition. The farmer is a price taker — they face a market price determined by the interaction of millions of buyers and sellers, and their only decision is how much to produce at that price. Understanding this decision requires combining the cost curves from Chapter 7 with the logic of profit maximisation.

Key Insight

A competitive firm's supply curve is not assumed — it is derived from its cost structure. Specifically, the firm's short-run supply curve is the portion of its marginal cost curve that lies above its average variable cost. The market supply curve is then the horizontal sum of all individual firms' supply curves. Entry and exit in the long run drive economic profit to zero, pinning the market price at the minimum of long-run average cost.

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to state the conditions for perfect competition, derive the profit-maximising output using the \(MR = MC\) rule, explain why a firm shuts down when price falls below AVC, trace the firm's short-run supply curve from its MC curve, and show how free entry drives the market to long-run equilibrium with zero economic profit.

8.1 Perfect Competition

Intuition: What Makes a Market Competitive?

A perfectly competitive market has four key characteristics: many buyers and many sellers (so no single agent can influence the price), a homogeneous product (one farmer's milk is indistinguishable from another's), free entry and exit (firms can enter when profits are positive and leave when they are negative), and perfect information (everyone knows the going price).

The critical implication is that each firm faces a perfectly elastic (horizontal) demand curve at the market price. The firm can sell as much as it wants at the market price, but nothing at all if it charges even a penny more — buyers would simply go elsewhere. Therefore, for a competitive firm, price equals marginal revenue: \(P = MR\). Each additional unit sold brings in exactly the market price.

Price Taker

A firm is a price taker if it cannot influence the market price by changing its own output. This happens when the firm's output is tiny relative to total market supply. The firm's demand curve is horizontal at the market price \(P\), and its total revenue is \(TR = P \times q\).

Are Real Markets Perfectly Competitive?

Very few markets satisfy all four conditions perfectly. But many come close enough for the model to be a good approximation: agricultural commodities (wheat, rice, milk), financial markets (shares of large companies), foreign exchange markets, and markets for raw materials (copper, oil on commodity exchanges). The model is also a powerful benchmark against which to measure the distortions caused by market power.

8.2 Profit Maximisation

Intuition: The MR = MC Rule

The firm's objective is to maximise profit: \(\pi = TR - TC\). It should produce an additional unit whenever the revenue from that unit (\(MR = P\)) exceeds the cost of producing it (\(MC\)). It should stop when \(MR = MC\). If it produced less, it would be leaving money on the table; if it produced more, each extra unit would cost more than it brings in.

Because \(MR = P\) for a competitive firm, the rule simplifies to: produce where \(P = MC\), provided MC is rising at that point (the second-order condition) and the firm does not prefer to shut down entirely.

Geometric Solution: TR, TC, and Profit

The upper panel shows total revenue (a straight line through the origin with slope \(P\)) and total cost. Profit is the vertical gap between them. The lower panel shows the profit curve directly. The profit-maximising output is where the gap is largest — equivalently, where the slopes of TR and TC are equal (\(P = MC\)).

Adjust the market price to see how the profit-maximising output changes. The firm produces where P = MC (slopes are equal).

Figure 8.1 — Upper: TR and TC. The profit-maximising output is where the vertical gap is largest. Lower: the profit curve peaks at that same output.

As you raise the price, the TR line rotates upward. The profit-maximising quantity increases (the firm moves up its MC curve), and maximum profit grows. As you lower the price, the TR line flattens, the optimal output shrinks, and eventually profit turns negative — the firm makes losses.

Losses Do Not Mean Immediate Shutdown

A firm earning negative profit in the short run does not necessarily shut down. If it shuts down, it still pays its fixed costs (FC) and earns zero revenue, for a loss of \(-FC\). If it continues operating, it loses \(TR - TC\). The firm should operate as long as \(TR - VC > 0\), i.e., as long as revenue covers variable costs. It shuts down only when \(P < AVC\).

8.3 The Firm's Short-Run Supply Curve

Intuition: MC Above AVC

The profit-maximisation rule says produce where \(P = MC\). But the firm has an outside option: produce nothing. If the price is so low that even at the optimal output the firm cannot cover its variable costs, it is better to shut down and lose only fixed costs. The shutdown price is the minimum of AVC — below this price, the firm produces zero.

Above the shutdown price, the firm's supply curve is its MC curve: for each price, the firm reads off the quantity where \(P = MC\). This gives us a remarkable result: the firm's short-run supply curve is the portion of the MC curve above the minimum of AVC.

Geometric Solution: Profit, Loss, and Shutdown

Drag the price to see how the firm responds. Green = profit, red = loss. Below the shutdown price, the firm produces zero.

Figure 8.2 — The firm's short-run supply decision. MC, ATC, and AVC curves with the profit/loss rectangle and shutdown price.

When \(P > ATC\), the firm earns positive economic profit (green rectangle). When \(AVC < P < ATC\), the firm operates at a loss but continues producing because revenue covers variable costs — shutting down would mean losing more (the full FC). When \(P < AVC\), the firm shuts down: producing anything would increase losses beyond the fixed-cost floor.

Three Price Zones

\(P > ATC\): Firm produces at \(P = MC\). Earns positive economic profit. Other firms want to enter.

\(AVC < P < ATC\): Firm produces at \(P = MC\). Makes a loss, but a smaller loss than if it shut down. Continues in the short run.

\(P < AVC\): Firm shuts down. Produces zero. Loses only FC.

8.4 Long-Run Equilibrium

Intuition: Entry, Exit, and Zero Profit

In the long run, firms can enter or exit the industry. If existing firms earn positive economic profit, new firms enter — attracted by the returns. Entry shifts the market supply curve rightward, pushing the price down. Firms keep entering until economic profit falls to zero. Conversely, if firms are making losses, some exit, supply shifts left, the price rises, and losses shrink until the remaining firms break even.

The long-run equilibrium condition is beautifully simple: \(P = \min(LRAC)\). Each firm produces at the minimum of its long-run average cost curve, earning zero economic profit. The market price is just high enough to cover all costs, including the opportunity cost of the owners' time and capital.

Long-Run Competitive Equilibrium

In long-run equilibrium: (1) each firm maximises profit: \(P = MC\); (2) each firm earns zero economic profit: \(P = ATC\); (3) the market clears: quantity supplied equals quantity demanded. Together, these imply \(P = MC = \min(LRAC)\).

Zero Profit Does Not Mean Zero Income

Students often react to "zero economic profit" with alarm: why would anyone run a business for zero profit? Remember that economic cost includes the owner's opportunity cost. A bakery owner earning zero economic profit is still paying themselves a salary equal to what they would earn elsewhere — they are doing exactly as well as their next-best option. Zero economic profit means "no incentive to enter or exit," not "no income."

8.5 Market Supply

Intuition: Adding Up Individual Firms

The market supply curve is the horizontal sum of all individual firms' supply curves. At each price, we add up the quantities that every firm wants to produce. With identical firms, this is simply \(Q_S = n \times q_i(P)\), where \(n\) is the number of firms and \(q_i(P)\) is the individual firm's supply at price \(P\).

In the short run, the number of firms is fixed, so the market supply curve is just a scaled-up version of the individual MC curve. In the long run, entry and exit change \(n\), making the long-run market supply curve potentially much flatter — even perfectly horizontal if all firms have identical costs (a constant-cost industry).

Geometric Solution: From Firm to Market

Change the number of firms to see how market supply shifts. Each firm produces at P = MC.

Figure 8.4 — Left: one firm's MC curve (its supply). Right: market supply = n × individual supply. More firms make the market supply flatter.

As the number of firms increases, the market supply curve becomes flatter — the market can absorb larger changes in demand with smaller price movements. In the extreme, with free entry and identical firms, the long-run supply curve is horizontal at \(P = \min(LRAC)\). In practice, industries with heterogeneous firms or scarce inputs (land, specialised labour) have upward-sloping long-run supply curves.

Industry Types by Long-Run Supply

Constant-cost industry: Entry does not affect input prices. LRAC is unchanged. Long-run supply is horizontal. Example: small-scale retail.

Increasing-cost industry: Entry bids up input prices (e.g., scarce skilled labour). LRAC shifts up with entry. Long-run supply slopes upward. Example: construction.

Decreasing-cost industry: Entry creates positive externalities (shared infrastructure, supplier networks). LRAC shifts down. Long-run supply slopes downward. Example: early tech clusters (Silicon Valley).

8.6 Formal Mathematical Derivations

We use the cost structure from Chapter 7 with \(q = L^{0.7}K^{0.3}\), \(\bar{K} = 10\), \(w = 10\), \(r = 5\). Recall that \(L = (q/\bar{K}^{0.3})^{1/0.7}\) and \(TC = 10L + 50\).

Profit Maximisation: First-Order Condition

The firm maximises:

\[ \pi(q) = Pq - C(q) \]

First-order condition:

\[ \frac{d\pi}{dq} = P - MC(q) = 0 \quad \Rightarrow \quad P = MC(q) \]

Second-order condition (ensures a maximum, not a minimum):

\[ \frac{d^2\pi}{dq^2} = -\frac{dMC}{dq} < 0 \quad \Rightarrow \quad MC \text{ must be rising} \]

For our cost function, \(MC = dTC/dq\). Using \(VC = wL = w(q/\bar{K}^{\beta})^{1/\alpha}\) with \(\alpha = 0.7\), \(\beta = 0.3\):

\[ VC(q) = 10 \cdot \left(\frac{q}{10^{0.3}}\right)^{10/7} \] \[ MC(q) = \frac{10}{0.7} \cdot \frac{1}{10^{3/7}} \cdot q^{3/7} \]

Since \(3/7 > 0\), MC is strictly increasing — the SOC is always satisfied.

The Supply Function

Inverting \(P = MC(q)\) gives the firm's supply function \(q^S(P)\):

\[ P = \frac{10}{0.7 \cdot 10^{3/7}} q^{3/7} \quad \Rightarrow \quad q^S(P) = \left(\frac{0.7 \cdot 10^{3/7}}{10} P\right)^{7/3} \]

This is an increasing function of \(P\) — the supply curve slopes upward, as expected.

Shutdown Condition

The firm operates if and only if \(P \geq \min(AVC)\). For our cost function:

\[ AVC = \frac{VC}{q} = \frac{10}{10^{3/7}} \cdot q^{3/7} \]

Since \(3/7 > 0\), AVC is strictly increasing for \(q > 0\), and \(\lim_{q \to 0^+} AVC = 0\). The minimum of AVC is effectively zero — the firm always operates at any positive price. (This is a feature of our specific functional form; with a cubic production function that allows an initial increasing-returns phase, AVC would have an interior minimum.)

Long-Run Equilibrium with Free Entry

In the long run with CRS (\(\alpha + \beta = 1\)), the long-run cost function from Chapter 7 is \(C^{LR} = 2q\sqrt{wr}\). With \(w = 10\), \(r = 5\):

\[ LRAC = \frac{C^{LR}}{q} = 2\sqrt{50} \approx 14.14 \]

LRAC is constant (flat), so the long-run equilibrium price is:

\[ P^* = \min(LRAC) = 2\sqrt{wr} = 2\sqrt{50} \approx £14.14 \]

Every firm earns zero economic profit. The number of firms adjusts to clear the market:

\[ n^* = \frac{Q^D(P^*)}{q^*(P^*)} \]

where \(Q^D\) is market demand and \(q^*\) is each firm's output at \(P^*\).

Market Supply Aggregation

With \(n\) identical firms, market supply is:

\[ Q^S(P) = n \cdot q^S(P) = n \left(\frac{0.7 \cdot 10^{3/7}}{10} P\right)^{7/3} \]

Market equilibrium requires \(Q^S(P) = Q^D(P)\). For a linear demand \(Q^D = a - bP\):

\[ n \left(\frac{0.7 \cdot 10^{3/7}}{10} P\right)^{7/3} = a - bP \]

This equation implicitly defines the equilibrium price as a function of demand parameters and the number of firms.

Comparative Statics: The Effect of a Cost Shock

Suppose the wage rises from \(w = 10\) to \(w' = 12\). The new LRAC is \(2\sqrt{12 \times 5} = 2\sqrt{60} \approx 15.49\). In the long run, the equilibrium price rises by approximately £1.35. Some firms exit (those who entered when the lower price prevailed), and the remaining firms each produce the same quantity at the higher price. The incidence of the cost shock depends on the elasticity of demand — connecting back to the tax incidence analysis of Chapter 3.

The Invisible Hand at Work

Adam Smith's "invisible hand" is formalised in the competitive model. No planner directs firms to produce at minimum cost, yet competition forces them to. No authority decrees the "right" number of firms, yet free entry and exit ensure exactly enough firms to satisfy demand at minimum average cost. Each firm pursues its own profit, yet the market outcome is efficient: the last unit produced costs exactly what consumers are willing to pay for it (\(P = MC\)), and no cheaper way to organise production exists (\(P = \min(LRAC)\)).

Summary

In this chapter, we combined the cost curves from Chapter 7 with the assumption of perfect competition to derive the firm's supply behaviour. A perfectly competitive firm is a price taker: it faces a horizontal demand curve at the market price and maximises profit by producing where \(P = MC\). The shutdown condition requires the price to be above the minimum of AVC — below this, the firm produces nothing. The firm's short-run supply curve is therefore its MC curve above min(AVC).

In the long run, free entry and exit drive economic profit to zero. The equilibrium price settles at the minimum of long-run average cost, and each firm produces at its most efficient scale. The number of firms adjusts until market supply exactly meets demand. The market supply curve is the horizontal sum of individual supply curves; in a constant-cost industry with free entry, it is perfectly elastic at \(P = \min(LRAC)\).

The competitive model is a benchmark of efficiency: production occurs at minimum cost, and the price reflects the true marginal cost of the last unit. In the next chapter, we use this benchmark to evaluate the welfare effects of government policies — taxes, subsidies, price controls, and trade restrictions.
