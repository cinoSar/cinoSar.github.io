The Problem: The Supermarket Price War

The UK grocery market is dominated by four firms — Tesco, Sainsbury’s, Asda, and Morrisons — which together hold roughly 65% of the market. When Aldi and Lidl entered with aggressive low-price strategies, the established players faced a dilemma: match the discounters’ prices and sacrifice margins, or hold the line and lose customers? Tesco eventually launched its own budget range. Sainsbury’s merged with Asda (though the deal was blocked on competition grounds). Every firm’s best strategy depended on what its rivals did.

This is the world of oligopoly — a market with a small number of firms whose decisions are strategically interdependent. Unlike the competitive firm (which ignores rivals) or the monopolist (which has none), each oligopolist must anticipate how competitors will react to its choices. This chapter introduces the major models of oligopoly behaviour and their close cousin, monopolistic competition.

Key Insight

The outcome in an oligopoly depends critically on what firms compete on (quantity or price), when they move (simultaneously or sequentially), and whether they can collude. The Cournot model (simultaneous quantity), Stackelberg model (sequential quantity), and Bertrand model (simultaneous price) each yield different equilibrium prices and quantities. Understanding which model fits a given industry is the economist’s key task.

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to derive best-response functions and Cournot–Nash equilibrium, explain the first-mover advantage in the Stackelberg model, state and interpret the Bertrand paradox, characterise long-run equilibrium under monopolistic competition, and analyse the incentive to collude and the incentive to cheat on a cartel agreement.

13.1 The Cournot Model

Intuition: Simultaneous Quantity Competition

In the Cournot model, two (or more) firms simultaneously choose how much to produce, and the market price is determined by total output via the demand curve. Each firm treats the other’s output as fixed and chooses its own output to maximise profit. The result is a Nash equilibrium: neither firm can do better by unilaterally changing its output, given what the other is producing.

Definition: Best-Response Function

Firm \(i\)’s best-response function (or reaction function) gives the profit-maximising output of firm \(i\) as a function of the other firm’s output: \(q_i^*(q_j)\). In the Cournot equilibrium, both firms are simultaneously on their best-response functions: \(q_1^* = BR_1(q_2^*)\) and \(q_2^* = BR_2(q_1^*)\).

The best-response functions slope downward: if your rival produces more, the residual demand left for you shrinks, so your optimal output falls. Outputs are strategic substitutes. The Cournot equilibrium lies at the intersection of the two best-response curves.

When both firms have equal costs, the equilibrium is symmetric: each produces the same quantity. If firm 1’s cost falls, its best-response curve shifts outward (it wants to produce more at every level of the rival’s output), so in equilibrium firm 1 produces more and firm 2 produces less. The total Cournot output lies between the monopoly quantity and the competitive quantity, and the Cournot price lies between the monopoly price and the competitive price.

Key Insight

With \(n\) symmetric Cournot firms, total output is \(Q = \frac{n}{n+1} Q_c\) where \(Q_c\) is the competitive output. As \(n \to \infty\), the Cournot outcome converges to perfect competition. With \(n = 1\) it is monopoly. Cournot oligopoly thus spans the entire range between the two extremes.

13.2 The Stackelberg Model

Intuition: First-Mover Advantage

What if one firm moves first? In the Stackelberg model, a leader chooses output before the follower, and the follower observes the leader’s choice before deciding its own output. The leader can exploit its first-mover advantage by committing to a large output, knowing the follower will accommodate by producing less.

The leader maximises profit by substituting the follower’s best-response function into its own profit function. Since it knows exactly how the follower will react, it effectively moves along the follower’s best-response curve, picking the point that maximises its own profit. The result: the leader produces more and earns more than in the Cournot equilibrium; the follower produces less and earns less.

Definition: Stackelberg Equilibrium

In a Stackelberg equilibrium, the leader chooses output to maximise profit taking the follower’s best-response function as given. The follower then best-responds to the leader’s observed output. The leader’s output exceeds its Cournot level; the follower’s output is below its Cournot level. Total output is higher (and price lower) than in Cournot.

Example: Airbus vs Boeing

The commercial aircraft market has long been a duopoly between Airbus and Boeing. When Airbus committed to developing the A380 super-jumbo, Boeing chose not to compete directly and instead developed the fuel-efficient 787 Dreamliner. Airbus’s large commitment acted as a Stackelberg move: by investing billions first, it shaped Boeing’s subsequent choice. (Whether it was a profitable move is another question — the A380 was eventually discontinued.)

13.3 The Bertrand Model

Intuition: Price Competition and the Bertrand Paradox

In the Bertrand model, firms compete on price rather than quantity. Each firm simultaneously announces a price, and the firm with the lower price captures the entire market (consumers buy from the cheapest source). If prices are equal, the market is split equally.

The striking result — the Bertrand paradox — is that with just two firms selling identical products, the equilibrium price equals marginal cost. Why? If either firm charged above MC, the other could undercut by a tiny amount and steal the entire market. This undercutting logic drives prices all the way down to MC, the competitive outcome, with just two firms.

Definition: Bertrand Paradox

The Bertrand paradox states that in a duopoly with homogeneous products, simultaneous price competition leads to the perfectly competitive outcome: \(P_1 = P_2 = MC\), with zero economic profit. Just two firms are enough to eliminate all market power — a dramatically different prediction from the Cournot model.

Geometric Solution: Price Undercutting

Figure 13.3 — Bertrand price competition. Best-response functions in price space. The equilibrium is at the intersection, where both firms price at (or just below) the higher MC.

The Bertrand paradox relies on strong assumptions: identical products, no capacity constraints, and consumers who switch entirely to the cheaper firm. Relaxing any of these changes the result. With product differentiation, each firm retains some customers even at a higher price, and the equilibrium price exceeds MC. With capacity constraints, firms cannot serve the entire market by undercutting, and outcomes resemble Cournot. In practice, most oligopolies have some differentiation and some capacity limits, so the Bertrand paradox is a useful benchmark rather than a literal prediction.

Which Model Is Right?

Cournot, Stackelberg, and Bertrand give different predictions for the same number of firms. The “right” model depends on the industry. Cournot fits industries where firms choose production capacity (oil, cement, steel). Bertrand fits industries where firms set prices with flexible output (retail, online markets). Stackelberg fits industries with a clear market leader (dominant firm with a competitive fringe). The choice of model is an empirical question.

13.4 Monopolistic Competition

Intuition: Differentiation Without Barriers

Monopolistic competition sits between perfect competition and monopoly. Like a competitive market, there are many firms and free entry/exit. Like a monopolist, each firm faces a downward-sloping demand curve because its product is slightly different from competitors’. Coffee shops in a city, restaurants on a high street, and clothing brands all operate in monopolistically competitive markets.

In the short run, a monopolistically competitive firm can earn positive or negative economic profit. In the long run, entry and exit drive economic profit to zero. New firms enter when profits are positive, shifting each existing firm’s demand curve inward (each firm gets a smaller share). Entry continues until each firm’s demand curve is just tangent to its LRAC curve — it charges a price equal to average cost and earns zero economic profit.

Definition: Monopolistic Competition Long-Run Equilibrium

In the long run, a monopolistically competitive firm produces where its demand curve is tangent to its long-run average cost curve. The firm charges \(P = LRAC\) (zero profit) but \(P > MC\) (the demand curve is downward-sloping). The firm operates with excess capacity: it produces less than the output that minimises LRAC.

Key Insight

Monopolistic competition involves a trade-off: consumers benefit from product variety (each firm offers something slightly different), but they pay for it through excess capacity (each firm produces below minimum efficient scale). Whether the variety benefit outweighs the efficiency cost is an empirical question that depends on consumer preferences for diversity.

13.5 Collusion and Cartels

Intuition: The Temptation to Cheat

If oligopolists could cooperate, they would jointly restrict output to the monopoly level and share the monopoly profit. A formal agreement to do so is a cartel. OPEC is the most famous example: oil-producing nations agree on production quotas to keep prices high.

The problem is that every cartel member has an individual incentive to cheat. If everyone else restricts output, the market price is high, and a cheater can produce more at that high price, earning extra profit at the cartel’s expense. This is a classic prisoners’ dilemma: collective restraint is profitable for the group, but individual defection is profitable for each member.

Definition: Cartel

A cartel is an agreement among competing firms to coordinate output or pricing decisions, typically to restrict total output and raise prices towards the monopoly level. Cartels are illegal in most jurisdictions (including the UK under the Competition Act 1998) because they harm consumers. Despite this, cartels are regularly discovered — the European Commission has fined truck manufacturers, banks, and pharmaceutical companies billions of euros for collusion.

Geometric Solution: Cartel vs Defection

The graph below compares three outcomes: the Cournot duopoly equilibrium, the cartel (joint monopoly) outcome, and the defection outcome where one firm cheats on the cartel whilst the other sticks to the agreement. Toggle between scenarios to see profits change.

Figure 13.5 — Cartel dynamics: compare Cournot equilibrium, cartel output, and the defection payoff. The defecting firm earns the highest individual profit.

The payoff ranking for each firm is: defect whilst the other cooperates > joint cartel > Cournot > both defect. This is exactly the prisoners’ dilemma structure. In a one-shot game, defection is the dominant strategy, so the cartel collapses to Cournot. Sustaining collusion requires repeated interaction — the threat of future punishment (a “price war”) can discipline current behaviour, provided firms are patient enough. We explore this formally in Chapter 14 on game theory.

Example: OPEC

OPEC regularly sets production quotas, yet members routinely exceed them. Saudi Arabia, as the largest producer, often acts as a swing producer — cutting its own output to maintain the cartel price even when smaller members cheat. This is consistent with the theory: the largest member has the most to lose from a price collapse and therefore the strongest incentive to enforce discipline, even at the cost of shouldering a disproportionate share of the output restriction.

13.6 Formal Derivations

Cournot Duopoly

Let inverse demand be \(P = a - b(q_1 + q_2)\) and let both firms have constant marginal cost \(c\). Firm 1’s profit is:

\[ \pi_1 = [a - b(q_1 + q_2)]q_1 - cq_1 \]

The first-order condition:

\[ \frac{\partial \pi_1}{\partial q_1} = a - 2bq_1 - bq_2 - c = 0 \quad \Rightarrow \quad q_1^* = \frac{a - c - bq_2}{2b} = \frac{a - c}{2b} - \frac{q_2}{2} \]

This is firm 1’s best-response function. By symmetry, \(q_2^* = (a - c)/(2b) - q_1/2\). In the symmetric equilibrium \(q_1^* = q_2^* = q^*\):

\[ q^* = \frac{a - c}{3b}, \quad Q^* = \frac{2(a - c)}{3b}, \quad P^* = \frac{a + 2c}{3} \]

Each firm’s profit is \(\pi^* = \frac{(a - c)^2}{9b}\). Compare with monopoly profit \(\pi_m = \frac{(a - c)^2}{4b}\): Cournot duopoly profit is less than half of monopoly profit, because firms do not internalise the externality of additional output on the rival’s revenue.

Stackelberg Duopoly

The leader (firm 1) substitutes the follower’s best response \(q_2 = (a - c - bq_1)/(2b)\) into its own profit:

\[ \pi_1 = \left[a - b\left(q_1 + \frac{a - c - bq_1}{2b}\right)\right]q_1 - cq_1 = \frac{(a - c)q_1 - bq_1^2}{2} \] \[ \frac{d\pi_1}{dq_1} = \frac{a - c - 2bq_1}{2} = 0 \quad \Rightarrow \quad q_1^L = \frac{a - c}{2b} \]

The follower produces \(q_2^F = (a - c)/(4b)\). Total output is \(Q = 3(a - c)/(4b)\), higher than Cournot. Leader profit is \((a - c)^2 / (8b)\), which exceeds Cournot profit \((a - c)^2 / (9b)\). Moving first is profitable.

Bertrand Duopoly

With identical products and equal MC = \(c\), the unique Nash equilibrium is \(P_1 = P_2 = c\). Any price above \(c\) is undercut by the rival; any price below \(c\) yields losses. With asymmetric costs \(c_1 < c_2\), the equilibrium has firm 1 pricing just below \(c_2\) and capturing the entire market.

Monopolistic Competition

Each firm \(i\) faces demand \(P_i = a_i - b_i q_i\) where \(a_i\) decreases as more firms enter (each firm’s market share shrinks). Long-run equilibrium requires zero profit: the demand curve is tangent to LRAC at the chosen output. This implies \(P = LRAC\) and \(MR = MC\) simultaneously, which in turn implies the firm operates on the downward-sloping portion of LRAC (excess capacity).

Cartel and Defection

Joint monopoly output: each firm produces \(q_m = (a - c) / (4b)\), earning \(\pi_m = (a - c)^2 / (8b)\). If firm 1 defects (best-responds to \(q_2 = q_m\)):

\[ q_1^D = \frac{a - c - bq_m}{2b} = \frac{a - c}{2b} - \frac{a - c}{8b} = \frac{3(a - c)}{8b} \] \[ \pi_1^D = \frac{9(a - c)^2}{64b} \]

Since \(9/64 > 1/8 = 8/64\), defection is profitable: \(\pi_1^D > \pi_m\). The temptation to cheat exceeds the reward from cooperation, confirming the prisoners’ dilemma structure.

Summary

Oligopoly is defined by strategic interdependence: each firm’s optimal decision depends on what its rivals do. The three canonical models — Cournot (simultaneous quantities), Stackelberg (sequential quantities), and Bertrand (simultaneous prices) — bracket the range of outcomes. Cournot yields prices and profits between monopoly and competition. Stackelberg gives the leader a first-mover advantage, producing more at the follower’s expense. Bertrand, with its paradoxical prediction that two firms suffice for the competitive outcome, highlights the importance of the competitive instrument (price vs quantity) and product differentiation.

Monopolistic competition combines elements of competition and monopoly: many firms, free entry, but differentiated products. The long-run tangency condition — demand tangent to LRAC — gives zero profit but excess capacity and a price above marginal cost. The trade-off is between variety (which consumers value) and productive efficiency (which excess capacity sacrifices).

Collusion is the ever-present temptation in oligopoly. Joint monopoly profits are large, but every cartel member has an individual incentive to cheat. This prisoners’ dilemma lies at the heart of competition policy: cartels are illegal precisely because they harm consumers, and antitrust authorities invest heavily in detecting and punishing them.
