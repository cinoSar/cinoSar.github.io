The Problem: Baristas or Machines?

Walk into any high-street coffee chain and you will see an expensive automated espresso machine doing most of the work. Walk into an independent specialty café and a skilled barista hand-pulls every shot. Both shops produce espresso — yet they use radically different combinations of labour and capital to do so.

Why? The answer cannot be that one method is simply "better." If automation were always superior, every shop would automate. If skilled labour were always preferable, nobody would buy machines. The choice depends on the technology available — how efficiently each combination of inputs produces output — and on the relative prices of labour and capital. To analyse this choice, we need a formal way to describe what a firm can produce: the production function.

Key Insight

The production function is to the firm what the utility function is to the consumer. Just as indifference curves map combinations of goods yielding equal satisfaction, isoquants map combinations of inputs yielding equal output. The tools of consumer theory — tangency conditions, constrained optimisation, substitution effects — carry over almost perfectly to the theory of the firm.

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to describe a firm's technology using a production function, distinguish between total, marginal, and average product of an input, explain the law of diminishing marginal returns, read and draw isoquant maps, compute the marginal rate of technical substitution (MRTS), and classify production technologies by their returns to scale.

6.1 Production Functions

Intuition: What Can a Firm Produce?

A firm takes inputs — workers (labour, \(L\)) and machinery (capital, \(K\)) — and transforms them into output (\(q\)). The production function \(q = f(L, K)\) tells us the maximum output achievable from any given combination of inputs, assuming the firm uses its inputs efficiently.

In the short run, at least one input is fixed. A coffee shop that has already leased its premises and bought its espresso machine can only vary the number of baristas. In the long run, all inputs are variable — the shop can relocate, buy more machines, or switch its entire setup.

Total, Marginal, and Average Product

Total product of labour \(TP_L = f(L, \bar{K})\): total output as a function of labour, holding capital fixed.

Marginal product of labour \(MP_L = \partial f / \partial L\): the additional output from one more unit of labour.

Average product of labour \(AP_L = f(L, \bar{K}) / L\): output per worker.

Initially, adding workers to a fixed amount of capital raises output rapidly — there is equipment sitting idle, and each new worker can be productively employed. But eventually, the workspace becomes crowded, workers get in each other's way, and each additional worker adds less and less to total output. This is the law of diminishing marginal returns: holding one input fixed, the marginal product of the variable input eventually declines.

Diminishing Returns ≠ Decreasing Output

Diminishing marginal returns means each extra worker adds less than the previous one, not that total output falls. Total output continues to rise as long as \(MP_L > 0\). It is only when the marginal product turns negative (extreme overcrowding) that adding workers actually reduces output.

6.2 Marginal and Average Product

Intuition: The Relationship Between MP and AP

Think of a cricket team's batting average. If a new batsman scores more than the team's current average, the average rises. If the new batsman scores less than the average, the average falls. Exactly the same logic applies to production: when the marginal product of the next worker exceeds the average product, the average rises; when it falls below, the average falls. This means \(MP_L\) must cross \(AP_L\) at the maximum of \(AP_L\).

The MP–AP Relationship

When \(MP_L > AP_L\), the average is rising. When \(MP_L < AP_L\), the average is falling. Therefore, \(MP_L = AP_L\) exactly at the point where \(AP_L\) is maximised. This crossing point tells the firm the most efficient level of labour per unit of output.

Geometric Solution: MP and AP Curves

The graph below shows the marginal and average product curves derived from the total product curve. The vertical dashed line marks the point where MP crosses AP — the maximum of AP.

Move L to see how MP and AP change. Notice that MP intersects AP at AP's peak.

Figure 6.2 — Marginal product (blue) and average product (green). MP crosses AP at the maximum of AP.

For the Cobb-Douglas function \(q = L^{0.7} K^{0.3}\), both curves decline throughout — there is no initial increasing phase because the exponent on labour (0.7) is less than 1. With other functional forms (such as a cubic production function), MP might first rise and then fall, creating a more pronounced intersection with AP. The key qualitative result — MP crosses AP at AP's peak — holds universally.

Why Firms Care About AP

Average product tells you output per worker — a measure of labour productivity. When a government reports that "manufacturing productivity grew by 2% this year," it is talking about average product. Firms care about AP because it determines unit labour costs: if the wage is \(w\), the labour cost per unit of output is \(w / AP_L\). Higher average product means lower unit costs.

6.3 Isoquants and the MRTS

Intuition: The Firm's Indifference Curves

An isoquant is a curve showing all combinations of labour and capital that produce the same level of output. It is the production counterpart of the consumer's indifference curve. Just as indifference curves are level sets of the utility function, isoquants are level sets of the production function.

Isoquants slope downward (if you use less capital, you need more labour to maintain the same output) and are convex to the origin (reflecting diminishing effectiveness of substitution). The slope of an isoquant is the marginal rate of technical substitution (MRTS) — the rate at which the firm can substitute labour for capital whilst keeping output constant.

Marginal Rate of Technical Substitution

The MRTS of labour for capital is:

\[ MRTS_{LK} = -\frac{dK}{dL}\bigg|_{q=\bar{q}} = \frac{MP_L}{MP_K} \]

It tells you how many units of capital the firm can give up when it hires one more unit of labour, holding output fixed. Analogous to the consumer's MRS.

Notice that as you move rightward along an isoquant (using more labour and less capital), the MRTS decreases. The isoquant becomes flatter because labour becomes relatively less effective at substituting for capital — there is already a lot of labour relative to capital, so the marginal product of labour is low and the marginal product of capital is high. This is the production analogue of the diminishing MRS in consumer theory.

Special Production Technologies

Perfect substitutes: \(q = aL + bK\). Isoquants are straight lines. The firm can freely swap labour for capital at a constant rate. Example: digging a trench with shovels or an excavator.

Perfect complements (Leontief): \(q = \min(aL, bK)\). Isoquants are L-shaped. Inputs must be used in fixed proportions. Example: one lorry needs exactly one driver.

Cobb-Douglas: \(q = A L^\alpha K^\beta\). Smooth, convex isoquants. The standard case used throughout this chapter.

6.4 Returns to Scale

Intuition: What Happens When You Scale Up Everything?

Diminishing marginal returns describes what happens when you increase one input whilst holding the other fixed. Returns to scale asks a different question: what happens when you increase all inputs proportionally?

If you double both labour and capital, does output exactly double (constant returns to scale), more than double (increasing returns to scale), or less than double (decreasing returns to scale)? The answer has profound implications for industry structure. If there are increasing returns to scale, larger firms are more efficient, and the industry will tend toward concentration. If there are decreasing returns, small firms can compete effectively.

Returns to Scale

For a production function \(q = f(L, K)\), multiply all inputs by \(t > 1\):

Increasing returns to scale (IRS): \(f(tL, tK) > t \cdot f(L, K)\). Output more than doubles.

Constant returns to scale (CRS): \(f(tL, tK) = t \cdot f(L, K)\). Output exactly doubles.

Decreasing returns to scale (DRS): \(f(tL, tK) < t \cdot f(L, K)\). Output less than doubles.

For Cobb-Douglas \(q = A L^\alpha K^\beta\): IRS if \(\alpha + \beta > 1\), CRS if \(\alpha + \beta = 1\), DRS if \(\alpha + \beta < 1\).

Geometric Solution: Isoquant Spacing and Returns to Scale

Returns to scale are visible in the spacing of isoquants along a ray from the origin. With CRS, isoquants for \(q = 1, 2, 3, \ldots\) are evenly spaced. With IRS, they get closer together (you need proportionally less extra input to get the next unit of output). With DRS, they spread apart.

Toggle between returns-to-scale regimes. The dots along the ray show where each isoquant is crossed — notice how the spacing changes.

Figure 6.4 — Returns to scale shown by isoquant spacing along a ray from the origin. CRS: even spacing. IRS: spacing narrows. DRS: spacing widens.

Under CRS, the isoquants are evenly spaced along any ray from the origin — doubling inputs exactly doubles output, so you travel the same distance to reach each successive isoquant. Under IRS, the isoquants bunch together — each doubling of output requires less than a doubling of inputs. Under DRS, the isoquants spread apart — you need ever more inputs to achieve each additional unit of output.

Returns to Scale in Practice

Increasing returns: Software development — writing code once and distributing it to millions costs almost nothing extra per user. Network platforms like social media exhibit extreme IRS.

Constant returns: Many manufacturing processes at moderate scale — a second identical factory produces the same output as the first.

Decreasing returns: Agriculture at very large scale — coordination costs, transport distances, and management complexity grow faster than output. Also common in service industries where personal attention matters (healthcare, education).

6.5 Technical Progress

Intuition: Getting More from the Same Inputs

Technical progress means producing the same output with fewer inputs, or more output from the same inputs. On an isoquant map, innovation shifts the isoquants inward toward the origin — the input combinations needed to produce a given output shrink.

Not all technical progress is created equal. Neutral progress reduces the need for both inputs proportionally. Labour-saving progress reduces the need for labour more than capital (think automation replacing workers). Capital-saving progress reduces the need for capital more than labour (think open-source software replacing expensive proprietary systems).

Types of Technical Progress

Neutral (Hicks-neutral): The production function becomes \(q = A(t) \cdot f(L, K)\), where \(A(t)\) increases over time. The MRTS at any given \(K/L\) ratio is unchanged — isoquants shrink symmetrically.

Labour-saving: The isoquant shifts inward more along the labour axis. At any given \(K/L\), the MRTS falls — labour becomes relatively less important.

Capital-saving: The isoquant shifts inward more along the capital axis. At any given \(K/L\), the MRTS rises — capital becomes relatively less important.

Geometric Solution: How Innovation Shifts Isoquants

Select a type of progress and increase the slider to see how the isoquant for q = 4 shifts inward.

Figure 6.5 — Technical progress shifts isoquants inward. The type of progress determines the direction of the shift.

Watch how neutral progress shrinks the isoquant symmetrically — the firm needs proportionally less of both inputs. Labour-saving progress pulls the isoquant primarily leftward (less labour needed), while capital-saving progress pulls it primarily downward (less capital needed). The practical implication: the direction of technical progress determines whether automation replaces workers or complements them.

The Direction of Progress Matters

Much of the debate about artificial intelligence and employment hinges on whether AI is labour-saving or labour-complementing. If AI is strongly labour-saving, firms can produce the same output with far fewer workers, putting downward pressure on wages. If it is neutral or capital-saving, workers benefit from higher productivity without displacement. The isoquant framework lets us reason precisely about these distinctions.

6.6 Formal Mathematical Derivations

This section collects the rigorous mathematical treatment of the production concepts developed intuitively above. We use the Cobb-Douglas production function \(q = A L^\alpha K^\beta\) as the running example, with the baseline case \(A = 1, \alpha = 0.5, \beta = 0.5\) so that \(q = \sqrt{LK}\).

Marginal and Average Product

For \(q = A L^\alpha K^\beta\):

\[ MP_L = \frac{\partial q}{\partial L} = \alpha A L^{\alpha - 1} K^\beta = \alpha \frac{q}{L} \] \[ MP_K = \frac{\partial q}{\partial K} = \beta A L^\alpha K^{\beta - 1} = \beta \frac{q}{K} \] \[ AP_L = \frac{q}{L} = A L^{\alpha - 1} K^\beta \]

Notice that \(MP_L = \alpha \cdot AP_L\). Since \(0 < \alpha < 1\), we have \(MP_L < AP_L\) for all \(L > 0\). For the standard Cobb-Douglas, MP is always below AP, and both decline monotonically. The crossing of MP and AP at AP's maximum is more visible with functional forms that allow an initial increasing phase (e.g., a cubic TP curve).

For the baseline case \(\alpha = \beta = 0.5\), \(K = 10\):

\[ q = \sqrt{10L}, \quad MP_L = \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{10}{L}}, \quad AP_L = \sqrt{\frac{10}{L}} \]

At \(L = 5\): \(q = \sqrt{50} \approx 7.07\), \(MP_L \approx 0.71\), \(AP_L \approx 1.41\).

The MRTS and Isoquants

The marginal rate of technical substitution is:

\[ MRTS_{LK} = \frac{MP_L}{MP_K} = \frac{\alpha K}{\beta L} \]

For \(\alpha = \beta = 0.5\): \(MRTS = K/L\). At the point \((L, K) = (4, 4)\) on the isoquant \(q = 4\): \(MRTS = 1\). This means the firm can give up 1 unit of capital for each extra unit of labour, maintaining the same output.

The isoquant equation (solving \(q = \sqrt{LK}\) for \(K\)):

\[ K = \frac{q^2}{L} \]

This is a rectangular hyperbola, exactly like the indifference curve for Cobb-Douglas utility. The analogy between consumer and producer theory is exact.

Returns to Scale

Multiply both inputs by \(t > 1\):

\[ f(tL, tK) = A (tL)^\alpha (tK)^\beta = A t^{\alpha + \beta} L^\alpha K^\beta = t^{\alpha + \beta} \cdot q \]

Therefore:

\[ f(tL, tK) = t^{\alpha + \beta} \cdot f(L, K) \]

The sum \(\alpha + \beta\) determines the returns to scale:

\[ \alpha + \beta > 1 \Rightarrow \text{IRS}, \quad \alpha + \beta = 1 \Rightarrow \text{CRS}, \quad \alpha + \beta < 1 \Rightarrow \text{DRS} \]

For our baseline \(\alpha = \beta = 0.5\): \(\alpha + \beta = 1\), so we have constant returns to scale. Doubling \(L\) and \(K\) exactly doubles \(q\).

Euler's Theorem and Factor Payments

For a CRS production function (\(\alpha + \beta = 1\)), Euler's theorem states:

\[ L \cdot MP_L + K \cdot MP_K = q \]

This is a remarkable result: if each input is paid its marginal product, the total payment to all inputs exactly exhausts total output. There is nothing left over — no "residual" or "economic profit." This provides a theoretical foundation for competitive factor pricing under CRS.

Proof for Cobb-Douglas:

\[ L \cdot MP_L + K \cdot MP_K = L \cdot \alpha \frac{q}{L} + K \cdot \beta \frac{q}{K} = (\alpha + \beta) q = q \quad \text{(when } \alpha + \beta = 1\text{)} \]

Elasticity of Substitution

The elasticity of substitution measures how easily the firm can substitute between inputs:

\[ \sigma = \frac{d\ln(K/L)}{d\ln(MRTS_{LK})} \]

For Cobb-Douglas: \(MRTS = (\alpha/\beta)(K/L)\), so \(\ln(MRTS) = \ln(\alpha/\beta) + \ln(K/L)\). Therefore:

\[ \sigma = \frac{d\ln(K/L)}{d\ln(K/L)} = 1 \]

The Cobb-Douglas elasticity of substitution is always 1, regardless of the parameter values. This means a 1% increase in the relative price of labour induces a 1% increase in the capital-to-labour ratio. More general functional forms — like the CES (constant elasticity of substitution) function \(q = A[\delta L^\rho + (1-\delta)K^\rho]^{1/\rho}\) — allow \(\sigma\) to take any positive value.

Technical Progress: Hicks-Neutral Formulation

With Hicks-neutral technical progress, the production function becomes:

\[ q = A(t) \cdot f(L, K) \]

where \(A(t)\) is the total factor productivity (TFP), increasing over time. The MRTS at any \(K/L\) ratio is unchanged because \(A(t)\) cancels in the ratio \(MP_L / MP_K\). For the growth rate of output:

\[ \frac{\dot{q}}{q} = \frac{\dot{A}}{A} + \alpha \frac{\dot{L}}{L} + \beta \frac{\dot{K}}{K} \]

This is the Solow growth accounting equation. The term \(\dot{A}/A\) — the growth rate of TFP — captures the contribution of technical progress that cannot be attributed to growth in measured inputs. It is often called the Solow residual.

Duality with Consumer Theory

The structural parallels are exact. The utility function \(U(q_1, q_2)\) corresponds to the production function \(q = f(L, K)\). Indifference curves correspond to isoquants. The MRS corresponds to the MRTS. The budget constraint corresponds to the isocost line (which we will meet in Chapter 7). The tangency condition \(MRS = p_1/p_2\) corresponds to \(MRTS = w/r\). And the Slutsky equation for consumers has an analogue in the firm's input demand response to factor price changes. Understanding one side of this duality makes the other almost immediate.

Summary

In this chapter, we moved from the consumer side of the market to the firm side. A firm's technology is captured by its production function \(q = f(L, K)\), which maps inputs to the maximum achievable output. In the short run, with capital fixed, the total product curve shows how output responds to changes in labour alone.

The marginal product of labour is the extra output from one additional worker and, thanks to the law of diminishing marginal returns, eventually declines as more labour is added to a fixed stock of capital. The marginal product always intersects the average product at the latter's maximum — a result that follows from simple algebra and holds for any production function.

Isoquants are the production counterpart of indifference curves: they show all input combinations yielding the same output. The slope of an isoquant is the MRTS, measuring how easily the firm can substitute one input for another. Returns to scale describe what happens when all inputs are increased proportionally: output may rise more than proportionally (IRS), exactly proportionally (CRS), or less than proportionally (DRS). For Cobb-Douglas production, the sum of the exponents \(\alpha + \beta\) determines the regime.

Finally, technical progress shifts isoquants inward, enabling the same output from fewer inputs. The direction of progress — neutral, labour-saving, or capital-saving — has important implications for labour markets and income distribution.
