The Problem: Why Are Footballers Paid Millions?

In 2023, the average Premier League footballer earned over £3 million per year. A nurse in the NHS earned roughly £35,000. Both work hard, both provide value to society, yet the pay gap is staggering. Why?

The answer lies not in moral judgements about “fairness” but in the economics of factor markets — the markets where firms buy the inputs (labour, capital, land) they use in production. A footballer’s wage is determined by the demand for footballers (driven by the enormous revenues of Premier League clubs) and the supply of world-class talent (extremely scarce). A nurse’s wage reflects the demand for nursing services (large but budget-constrained) and the supply of qualified nurses (also large). Factor prices are determined by the same supply-and-demand logic as output prices, but with an important twist: the demand for factors is derived from the demand for the goods they produce.

Key Insight

The demand for a factor of production is a derived demand: it comes not from the factor itself but from the output it helps produce. A firm hires labour up to the point where the marginal revenue product (MRP) — the extra revenue from hiring one more unit — equals the factor price. When the factor market is competitive, the wage equals MRP. When the employer has market power (monopsony), the wage falls below MRP, creating a deadweight loss.

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to derive the demand for labour from the marginal revenue product, explain the income-leisure trade-off and the backward-bending labour supply curve, compare competitive and monopsony labour market equilibria, analyse the demand for capital using the user cost of capital, and apply factor market analysis to real-world wage differentials.

15.1 Demand for Labour

Intuition: Hiring Until MRP = w

Why does a firm hire workers? Because each worker produces output that the firm can sell. The marginal revenue product of labour (MRPL) is the additional revenue the firm earns from hiring one more worker: MRPL = MR × MPL. For a competitive firm (where MR = P), this simplifies to MRPL = P × MPL, which is called the value of the marginal product (VMPL).

Definition: Marginal Revenue Product

The marginal revenue product of labour is \(\text{MRP}_L = MR \times MP_L\). For a competitive firm, \(\text{MRP}_L = P \times MP_L = \text{VMP}_L\). The firm’s demand curve for labour is its MRPL curve: it hires workers up to the point where \(\text{MRP}_L = w\), the market wage.

Because of diminishing marginal returns (Chapter 6), MPL declines as more workers are hired. This makes the MRP curve downward-sloping — the firm’s demand for labour is a decreasing function of the wage. If the output price rises, the entire MRP curve shifts up, and the firm hires more workers at any given wage.

Example: Why Tech Workers Earn More

Software engineers command high salaries because the output price of software (revenue per user) is high and the marginal product of a skilled engineer is substantial. A single developer can build features used by millions. The MRP is enormous, and wages reflect that. By contrast, in industries with low output prices or easily substitutable labour, MRP and wages are lower.

15.2 Supply of Labour

Intuition: The Income–Leisure Trade-Off

Workers choose how many hours to work by trading off income (from working) against leisure (the time not spent working). A higher wage has two effects. The substitution effect makes leisure more expensive (each hour of leisure “costs” more in forgone wages), encouraging more work. The income effect makes the worker richer, and if leisure is a normal good, the worker wants more of it — encouraging less work.

At low wages, the substitution effect dominates: higher wages lead to more hours worked, and the labour supply curve slopes upward. But at high wages, the income effect can dominate: workers feel rich enough to “buy” more leisure, and hours worked fall. The result is a backward-bending labour supply curve.

Definition: Backward-Bending Labour Supply

The backward-bending labour supply curve slopes upward at low wages (substitution effect dominates) and bends backward at high wages (income effect dominates). The worker supplies fewer hours as the wage increases beyond a certain point, because the additional income is worth less than the leisure it displaces.

Market vs Individual Supply

Even if individual labour supply curves bend backward, the market supply curve typically slopes upward throughout the relevant range. Higher wages attract new workers into the labour force (people who were not working before), offsetting the backward-bending behaviour of those already employed. The market supply of labour is the horizontal sum of all individual supply curves plus the entry margin.

15.3 Labour Market Equilibrium

Intuition: Competition vs Monopsony

In a competitive labour market, many firms compete for workers. Each firm is a wage taker: it hires at the market wage where the total demand for labour (sum of all firms’ MRP curves) equals total supply. The equilibrium wage equals the MRP — workers are paid what they are worth at the margin.

A monopsony is a market with a single buyer of labour. Just as a monopolist restricts output to raise the price, a monopsonist restricts hiring to lower the wage. But hiring one more worker requires raising the wage for all workers (since the supply curve slopes upward), making the marginal expenditure on labour (MEL) greater than the wage. The monopsonist hires where MRP = ME and pays the wage read off the supply curve — below MRP.

Definition: Monopsony

A monopsony is a market with a single buyer. The monopsonist’s marginal expenditure exceeds the supply price because hiring an additional unit requires raising the price paid to all units. The monopsony equilibrium has a lower quantity and lower price than the competitive equilibrium, creating a deadweight loss.

Geometric Solution: Competitive vs Monopsony

Toggle between competitive and monopsony equilibrium. Under monopsony, the ME curve lies above the supply curve, and the firm hires fewer workers at a lower wage than the competitive outcome.

Figure 15.3 — Competitive vs monopsony labour market. Under monopsony, MEL > w, and the firm hires fewer workers at a lower wage.

Example: The NHS as Monopsonist

The National Health Service is by far the largest employer of nurses in the UK. Economic studies have found evidence of monopsony power: nurses’ wages are below their MRP, and employment is below the competitive level. This is one reason governments periodically raise NHS pay bands — a minimum wage in a monopsony market can actually increase both employment and wages simultaneously, a result impossible in a competitive market.

15.4 Capital Markets

Intuition: Renting Machines

Just as firms hire labour, they also use capital — machines, buildings, equipment. The demand for capital follows the same logic: a firm employs capital up to the point where the marginal revenue product of capital (MRPK) equals the rental rate (r), the cost of using one unit of capital for one period.

Definition: User Cost of Capital

The user cost of capital is the total cost of using one unit of capital for one period. It includes the interest cost (opportunity cost of the funds tied up), depreciation, and any expected capital gains or losses: \(r = i \cdot P_K + \delta \cdot P_K - \Delta P_K\), where \(i\) is the interest rate, \(\delta\) is the depreciation rate, and \(\Delta P_K\) is the expected price change.

The firm’s demand curve for capital is its MRPK curve, which slopes downward due to diminishing marginal returns. A lower rental rate encourages firms to use more capital-intensive techniques; a higher rate pushes them towards labour-intensive methods. In long-run equilibrium, the ratio of factor prices equals the ratio of marginal products: \(w / r = MP_L / MP_K\) — the familiar cost-minimisation condition from Chapter 7.

Key Insight

Factor markets are mirrors of output markets. The demand for each factor is derived from the demand for the output it produces. In competitive factor markets, each factor is paid its marginal revenue product. In the long run, firms choose the capital–labour ratio that minimises cost, giving the tangency condition \(\text{MRTS} = w/r\) from Chapter 7. Factor prices thus determine not only how much of each factor is used but how goods are produced.

15.5 Formal Derivations

Competitive Labour Demand

A competitive firm produces \(q = f(L, \bar{K})\) and sells at price \(P\). Profit is:

\[ \pi = P \cdot f(L, \bar{K}) - wL - r\bar{K} \]

The first-order condition for labour:

\[ \frac{\partial \pi}{\partial L} = P \cdot MP_L - w = 0 \quad \Rightarrow \quad P \cdot MP_L = w \quad \Rightarrow \quad \text{VMP}_L = w \]

For a Cobb-Douglas production function \(q = L^{\alpha}\), \(MP_L = \alpha L^{\alpha - 1}\), so the demand for labour is:

\[ P \cdot \alpha L^{\alpha - 1} = w \quad \Rightarrow \quad L^* = \left(\frac{\alpha P}{w}\right)^{1/(1-\alpha)} \]

Monopsony

The monopsonist faces an upward-sloping supply curve \(w = a + bL\). Total expenditure on labour is \(wL = aL + bL^2\). Marginal expenditure is:

\[ ME_L = \frac{d(wL)}{dL} = a + 2bL \]

The ME curve has the same intercept as the supply curve but twice the slope — exactly analogous to the relationship between demand and MR for a monopolist. The monopsonist hires where \(\text{MRP}_L = ME_L\):

\[ P \cdot MP_L = a + 2bL_m \]

and pays the wage \(w_m = a + bL_m\), which is below the MRP. The deadweight loss triangle has base \(L_c - L_m\) and height \(w_c - w_m\), where subscripts \(c\) and \(m\) denote competitive and monopsony outcomes.

Backward-Bending Labour Supply

The worker maximises \(U(\text{Consumption}, \text{Leisure})\) subject to \(C = w(T - \ell) + Y_0\), where \(T\) is total time, \(\ell\) is leisure, and \(Y_0\) is non-labour income. Hours worked are \(h = T - \ell\). Using Cobb-Douglas preferences \(U = C^{\beta} \ell^{1-\beta}\):

\[ \ell^* = (1-\beta) \cdot \frac{wT + Y_0}{w}, \qquad h^* = T - \ell^* = \beta T - (1-\beta)\frac{Y_0}{w} \]

When \(Y_0 = 0\), \(h^* = \beta T\) is constant — Cobb-Douglas preferences give a vertical supply curve. With \(Y_0 > 0\), raising \(w\) increases \(h\) (the second term shrinks). To generate backward bending, we need non-homothetic preferences or the “target income” model where workers have a target earnings level.

User Cost of Capital

A firm that owns a machine with purchase price \(P_K\), interest rate \(i\), depreciation rate \(\delta\), and expected price appreciation \(g = \Delta P_K / P_K\) faces a user cost:

\[ r = (i + \delta - g) \cdot P_K \]

The firm employs capital where \(\text{MRP}_K = r\). With \(q = K^{\gamma}\), \(MP_K = \gamma K^{\gamma - 1}\), and the optimal capital stock is:

\[ K^* = \left(\frac{\gamma P}{r}\right)^{1/(1-\gamma)} \]

A fall in the interest rate reduces \(r\) and increases the desired capital stock — this is the channel through which monetary policy stimulates investment.

Summary

Factor markets determine the prices of labour, capital, and other inputs. The central principle is derived demand: the demand for a factor comes from the demand for the output it helps produce. A competitive firm hires each factor up to the point where its marginal revenue product equals its price. This explains why footballers earn millions (enormous MRP driven by huge revenues and scarce talent) whilst equally hardworking nurses earn far less (large supply relative to budget-constrained demand).

Labour supply reflects the trade-off between income and leisure. At low wages, the substitution effect dominates and supply slopes upward; at high wages, the income effect can dominate, producing the backward-bending curve. Market supply is typically upward-sloping because higher wages attract new entrants even when some individuals reduce their hours.

Monopsony — a single buyer of labour — is the factor-market analogue of monopoly. The monopsonist restricts hiring to push down wages, creating a deadweight loss. Interestingly, a well-designed minimum wage can increase both employment and wages in a monopsony market, a result with important implications for labour policy.
