The Problem: Why Do Students Pay Less?

Walk into a Vue cinema on a Tuesday afternoon and you will see two prices on the board: £12 for adults, £6 for students. The film is the same, the seat is the same, the popcorn is equally overpriced. Why does the cinema charge different customers different prices for an identical product?

Airlines do something even more dramatic. A London–Edinburgh return flight might cost £60 if booked six weeks ahead but £300 the day before departure. The plane, the route, and the service are identical. The only difference is the type of customer: leisure travellers book early and are price-sensitive; business travellers book late and are not.

In Chapter 11, we assumed the monopolist charges a single price to all customers. But a firm with market power can often do better by charging different prices to different consumers or for different units — a practice called price discrimination. This chapter explores the three degrees of price discrimination and their welfare consequences, then turns to advertising as a second tool for increasing profits.

Key Insight

Price discrimination allows a firm with market power to capture surplus that would otherwise go to consumers. First-degree (perfect) PD extracts all consumer surplus. Third-degree PD charges different prices to identifiable groups. Second-degree PD uses quantity discounts or menus of contracts to induce consumers to self-select. In some cases, price discrimination can actually increase total welfare by bringing more consumers into the market.

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to explain why price discrimination requires market power and the ability to segment customers, derive the optimal third-degree price discrimination strategy using the MR₁ = MR₂ = MC condition, design a two-part tariff that captures surplus from heterogeneous consumers, analyse how advertising shifts demand and when it is profitable, and apply the Dorfman–Steiner condition to determine optimal advertising expenditure.

12.1 First-Degree Price Discrimination

Intuition: Charging Each Consumer Their Maximum Willingness to Pay

Under first-degree (or perfect) price discrimination, the firm charges each consumer exactly their maximum willingness to pay for each unit. This means every unit is sold at a different price, and the firm captures the entire area between the demand curve and the marginal cost curve as profit. No consumer surplus remains.

Definition: First-Degree Price Discrimination

First-degree price discrimination occurs when a firm charges each consumer the maximum price they are willing to pay for each unit consumed. The firm’s marginal revenue equals the price (since no price reduction is needed on earlier units), so the firm produces the competitive quantity where P = MC. Total surplus is maximised, but it all accrues to the firm.

Perfect PD is largely a theoretical benchmark — it requires the firm to know every consumer’s exact willingness to pay, which is rarely feasible. But it is a useful reference point: it shows the maximum profit a firm with market power could extract, and it demonstrates that price discrimination can eliminate the deadweight loss of monopoly (at the cost of eliminating consumer surplus too).

Geometric Solution: Perfect PD vs Uniform Pricing

Toggle between uniform monopoly pricing and perfect price discrimination. Under uniform pricing, the familiar DWL triangle appears. Under perfect PD, the firm captures the entire surplus triangle — output expands to the competitive level and DWL disappears.

Figure 12.1 — Uniform monopoly pricing vs first-degree price discrimination. Under perfect PD, the firm captures the entire surplus and DWL disappears.

Welfare Paradox

Perfect price discrimination is Pareto efficient — there is no deadweight loss, and total surplus is maximised. Yet consumers are worse off than under uniform monopoly pricing, because they lose all their surplus. Efficiency and consumer welfare can move in opposite directions, which is why competition authorities care about both.

12.2 Third-Degree Price Discrimination

Intuition: Segmenting the Market

Third-degree price discrimination is the most common form in practice. The firm identifies two or more groups of consumers with different demand elasticities and charges each group a different price. Students and adults at the cinema, peak and off-peak rail fares, and domestic vs international drug prices are all examples.

The key requirement is that the firm must be able to identify which group a customer belongs to (using student cards, booking dates, geographic location) and prevent resale between groups. If students could resell their cheap tickets to adults, the pricing scheme would collapse.

Definition: Third-Degree Price Discrimination

Under third-degree price discrimination, the firm divides consumers into identifiable groups with different demand curves and charges each group a separate profit-maximising price. The optimal rule is \(MR_1 = MR_2 = \ldots = MC\): equalise marginal revenue across all markets and set it equal to marginal cost.

The group with less elastic demand pays the higher price. This follows from the inverse elasticity rule: \(P_i(1 + 1/\varepsilon_i) = MC\), so the price is higher when \(|\varepsilon_i|\) is smaller. Business travellers with inelastic demand for last-minute flights pay more than leisure travellers who can easily switch dates.

Geometric Solution: Two-Market Price Discrimination

The graph below shows a monopolist selling in two separate markets. Market 1 (e.g. adults) has demand \(P_1 = a_1 - b_1 Q_1\) and Market 2 (e.g. students) has demand \(P_2 = a_2 - b_2 Q_2\). Adjust the demand intercepts to change the relative elasticities. The firm equates MR across markets and sets each equal to MC.

Figure 12.2 — Third-degree price discrimination in two markets. The firm charges a higher price in the market with less elastic demand (higher intercept).

Key Insight

Third-degree PD always increases the firm’s profit relative to uniform pricing (otherwise the firm would just charge a single price). Its effect on total welfare is ambiguous: if PD opens a market that would otherwise be unserved (e.g. a student market), total surplus rises. If it merely redistributes sales between markets without changing total output, total surplus may fall.

12.3 Second-Degree Price Discrimination

Intuition: Letting Consumers Self-Select

Sometimes the firm cannot directly observe which type a consumer is. It cannot check whether a customer is price-sensitive or not. Instead, the firm offers a menu of pricing options and lets consumers reveal their type through their choices. This is second-degree price discrimination, also called nonlinear pricing.

Common examples include quantity discounts (“buy three, get one free”), block tariffs (electricity priced at different rates for different usage tiers), versioning (a basic vs premium product), and the quintessential tool: the two-part tariff.

Definition: Two-Part Tariff

A two-part tariff consists of a fixed fee \(T\) (an entry or access charge) plus a per-unit price \(p\). Total expenditure for a consumer buying \(q\) units is \(T + pq\). By setting the per-unit price and the fixed fee strategically, the firm can extract more surplus than uniform pricing.

With a single consumer type, the optimal two-part tariff is simple: set \(p = MC\) to maximise the surplus pie, then set \(T\) equal to the consumer’s entire consumer surplus to capture it all. With heterogeneous consumers, the design becomes more subtle — the firm must ensure that high-value consumers don’t switch to the low-value plan.

Example: Gym Memberships

A gym charges a monthly membership fee (the fixed part) plus pay-per-class fees (the per-unit part). Frequent exercisers have high demand and are willing to pay more in total. The gym sets the class fee low to encourage high usage, then captures surplus through the membership fee. Some gyms offer “unlimited” memberships (effectively \(p = 0\), high \(T\)) alongside pay-as-you-go options — a classic second-degree PD menu.

12.4 Advertising

Intuition: Shifting Demand

Price discrimination takes the demand curve as given and extracts more from it. Advertising takes a different approach: it tries to shift the demand curve outward. If successful, the firm can sell more at any given price — or charge a higher price for the same quantity.

Economists distinguish two types of advertising. Informative advertising tells consumers about a product’s existence, features, or price. It helps markets work better by reducing search costs. Persuasive advertising aims to change consumers’ preferences — to make them want the product more, independent of its objective characteristics. Most real advertising contains elements of both.

Definition: Dorfman–Steiner Condition

The Dorfman–Steiner condition states that the profit-maximising ratio of advertising expenditure to sales revenue equals the ratio of the advertising elasticity of demand to the price elasticity of demand:

\[\frac{A}{PQ} = \frac{\varepsilon_A}{|\varepsilon_P|}\]

where \(A\) is advertising expenditure, \(\varepsilon_A\) is the advertising elasticity, and \(\varepsilon_P\) is the price elasticity of demand. Firms should spend more on advertising when demand is responsive to advertising and when consumers are relatively price-insensitive.

Geometric Solution: Advertising and Profit

The graph below shows a monopolist that can spend on advertising to shift its demand curve outward. The advertising intensity slider controls how much the demand intercept increases (at a cost). The firm still sets MR = MC at the new demand curve. Observe the trade-off: advertising raises revenue but also costs money.

Figure 12.4 — The effect of advertising on demand, output, and profit. Advertising shifts demand outward but incurs a cost. The firm chooses the level of advertising that maximises profit net of advertising expenditure.

At zero advertising, the firm earns its baseline monopoly profit. As advertising increases from zero, profit rises (the demand shift more than covers the cost). Beyond a point, the incremental revenue from further demand shifts no longer justifies the cost, and net profit declines. The optimal advertising level is where the marginal revenue from advertising equals its marginal cost — one more pound spent on advertising raises revenue by exactly one pound.

Welfare Effects of Advertising

The welfare effects of advertising are contested. Informative advertising generally improves welfare by helping consumers make better choices. Persuasive advertising is more ambiguous: it may create “artificial” demand or it may reflect genuine value that consumers place on brand image, status, or reassurance. The debate turns on whether advertising-shifted preferences are “real” — a philosophical question that economics alone cannot resolve.

12.5 Formal Derivations

Third-Degree Price Discrimination

Suppose a monopolist sells in two markets with inverse demands \(P_1 = a_1 - b_1 Q_1\) and \(P_2 = a_2 - b_2 Q_2\), and has constant marginal cost \(c\). The firm maximises:

\[ \pi = (a_1 - b_1 Q_1)Q_1 + (a_2 - b_2 Q_2)Q_2 - c(Q_1 + Q_2) \]

First-order conditions:

\[ \frac{\partial \pi}{\partial Q_1} = a_1 - 2b_1 Q_1 - c = 0 \quad \Rightarrow \quad Q_1^* = \frac{a_1 - c}{2b_1} \] \[ \frac{\partial \pi}{\partial Q_2} = a_2 - 2b_2 Q_2 - c = 0 \quad \Rightarrow \quad Q_2^* = \frac{a_2 - c}{2b_2} \]

The prices are \(P_1^* = (a_1 + c)/2\) and \(P_2^* = (a_2 + c)/2\). Since \(a_1 > a_2\), we have \(P_1^* > P_2^*\): the market with higher willingness to pay is charged more. The MR condition \(MR_1 = MR_2 = c\) is satisfied in each market independently because costs are separable.

Optimal Two-Part Tariff

Consider two consumer types, H and L, with demand \(P_H = a_H - Q_H\) and \(P_L = a_L - Q_L\) where \(a_H > a_L\). There are \(n_H\) high types and \(n_L\) low types. Marginal cost is \(c\).

Case 1: Serve both types. The fee must not exceed the low type’s consumer surplus at the per-unit price: \(T \leq CS_L(p) = \frac{1}{2}(a_L - p)^2\). The firm sets \(T = CS_L(p)\) and maximises:

\[ \pi = (n_H + n_L) \cdot \frac{(a_L - p)^2}{2} + (p - c)\bigl[n_H(a_H - p) + n_L(a_L - p)\bigr] \]

Taking the FOC with respect to \(p\) and solving gives a per-unit price \(p^* > c\) when consumer types are heterogeneous. The fee distorts the per-unit price above MC to increase the fixed fee revenue.

Case 2: Serve only high types. Set \(T = CS_H(p) = \frac{1}{2}(a_H - p)^2\) and \(p = c\). Profit is \(n_H \cdot \frac{(a_H - c)^2}{2}\). The firm compares this to Case 1 profit and chooses the more profitable strategy.

The Dorfman–Steiner Condition

Let demand be \(Q = D(P, A)\) where \(A\) is advertising expenditure. The firm maximises \(\pi = PQ - C(Q) - A\). FOCs with respect to \(P\) and \(A\):

\[ \frac{\partial \pi}{\partial P}: \quad Q + (P - MC)\frac{\partial Q}{\partial P} = 0 \] \[ \frac{\partial \pi}{\partial A}: \quad (P - MC)\frac{\partial Q}{\partial A} - 1 = 0 \]

Dividing the second equation by the first (after rearranging):

\[ \frac{A}{PQ} = \frac{\varepsilon_A}{|\varepsilon_P|} \]

where \(\varepsilon_A = (\partial Q / \partial A)(A / Q)\) and \(\varepsilon_P = (\partial Q / \partial P)(P / Q)\). This is the Dorfman–Steiner condition. It says the advertising-to-sales ratio is higher when advertising is effective (\(\varepsilon_A\) large) and when consumers are price-insensitive (\(|\varepsilon_P|\) small).

Bundling

A firm selling two products can sometimes increase profit by offering them as a bundle. Suppose consumer A values product X at £10 and product Y at £3, whilst consumer B values X at £4 and Y at £9. With separate pricing, the best the firm can do is charge £4 for X and £3 for Y (to sell to both), earning £14. But if it offers a bundle at £13, both consumers buy (A values the bundle at £13, B at £13), and the firm earns £26. Bundling works when valuations are negatively correlated: high-X consumers tend to be low-Y consumers and vice versa, so the bundle valuation is more uniform than individual valuations.

Summary

A firm with market power has tools beyond simple monopoly pricing. First-degree price discrimination — charging each consumer their exact willingness to pay — captures all consumer surplus and, perhaps surprisingly, eliminates the deadweight loss of monopoly. It is the theoretical maximum of what a firm can extract, though it requires impossibly detailed knowledge of consumer preferences.

Third-degree price discrimination is far more common. By segmenting consumers into identifiable groups and charging each a different price, the firm exploits differences in demand elasticity. The inverse elasticity rule governs the price in each segment: the group with less elastic demand pays more. Third-degree PD always raises the firm’s profit; its effect on total welfare depends on whether it brings new consumers into the market.

Second-degree price discrimination uses self-selection mechanisms — quantity discounts, versioning, and two-part tariffs — to sort consumers who cannot be directly identified. The two-part tariff is a particularly powerful tool: by separating the per-unit price from the access fee, the firm can extract surplus whilst potentially maintaining efficient per-unit pricing. Advertising complements pricing strategy by shifting demand outward; the Dorfman–Steiner condition tells us how much a firm should spend on advertising as a share of revenue.
