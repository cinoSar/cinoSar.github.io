The Problem: How Do Consumers Choose?

You walk into a supermarket with £30 in your pocket. You fancy both pizza and beer, but you cannot afford unlimited amounts of either. Pizza costs £5, beer costs £3. How do you divide your budget? Do you buy six pizzas and no beer? Ten beers and no pizza? Some combination?

Most people would choose a mix — perhaps three pizzas and five beers. But why that particular combination? What principle guides the decision? And what happens to your choice if the price of beer doubles, or if you get a pay rise?

Key Insight

Consumer choice theory answers these questions by combining two building blocks: preferences (what consumers want) and the budget constraint (what they can afford). The optimal choice is the most-preferred bundle that fits within the budget. This framework is the foundation of the demand curve we studied in Chapter 2.

By the end of this chapter, you will understand how economists model preferences with indifference curves, how budget constraints limit choices, and how the optimal consumption bundle is determined — giving you the ability to predict how consumers respond to price and income changes.

4.1 Preferences and Indifference Curves

Intuition: Ranking Bundles

A consumer's preferences are simply their ability to rank different bundles of goods. Given a choice between "3 pizzas + 5 beers" and "4 pizzas + 2 beers", you either prefer one to the other, or you are indifferent between them. Economists assume preferences have three properties:

Assumptions about Preferences

Completeness: For any two bundles A and B, the consumer can say "I prefer A", "I prefer B", or "I am indifferent." No bundle is unrankable.

Transitivity: If A is preferred to B and B is preferred to C, then A is preferred to C. Preferences are logically consistent.

More is better (non-satiation): Holding everything else constant, having more of a good is at least weakly preferred. A bundle with more pizza and the same amount of beer is at least as good.

An indifference curve connects all the bundles that give the consumer the same level of satisfaction. If you are equally happy with "6 pizzas + 1 beer" and "2 pizzas + 4 beers", both points lie on the same indifference curve.

Indifference curves have important properties: they slope downward (to keep satisfaction constant, gaining more of one good requires giving up some of the other), they cannot cross (which would violate transitivity), and higher curves represent higher satisfaction (more is better).

Marginal Rate of Substitution (MRS)

The MRS is the rate at which a consumer is willing to trade one good for another while remaining equally satisfied. Geometrically, it is the slope of the indifference curve (in absolute value). The MRS typically diminishes as we move along an indifference curve: the more pizza you already have, the less beer you are willing to give up for yet another pizza.

Mathematical Formalisation

Along an indifference curve, utility is constant: \(U(q_1, q_2) = \bar{U}\). Taking the total differential and setting it to zero:

\[ dU = \frac{\partial U}{\partial q_1} dq_1 + \frac{\partial U}{\partial q_2} dq_2 = 0 \]

Rearranging gives the MRS:

\[ \text{MRS} = -\frac{dq_2}{dq_1} = \frac{\partial U / \partial q_1}{\partial U / \partial q_2} = \frac{MU_1}{MU_2} \]

where \(MU_1\) and \(MU_2\) are the marginal utilities of goods 1 and 2. The MRS equals the ratio of marginal utilities.

For a Cobb-Douglas utility function \(U = q_1^a \cdot q_2^b\):

\[ MU_1 = a \, q_1^{a-1} q_2^b, \quad MU_2 = b \, q_1^a q_2^{b-1} \] \[ \text{MRS} = \frac{a \, q_1^{a-1} q_2^b}{b \, q_1^a q_2^{b-1}} = \frac{a}{b} \cdot \frac{q_2}{q_1} \]

For \(U = q_1 \cdot q_2\) (where \(a = b = 1\)): \(\text{MRS} = q_2 / q_1\). At the point \((3, 3)\), the MRS is 1 — the consumer would trade pizza and beer one-for-one. At \((6, 1.5)\), the MRS is \(1.5/6 = 0.25\) — the consumer, flush with pizza, would give up only 0.25 beers for another pizza.

4.2 Utility Functions

Intuition: Putting a Number on Satisfaction

An indifference curve tells us that certain bundles are equally good, but it does not tell us how much better one curve is than another. A utility function assigns a number to every bundle such that preferred bundles get higher numbers. It is a convenient mathematical shorthand for the entire preference ordering.

An important subtlety: the level of utility has no meaning in itself. Saying "bundle A gives utility 10 and bundle B gives utility 5" does not mean A is twice as good. It only means A is preferred. Any monotonic transformation of a utility function represents the same preferences — \(U\), \(2U + 7\), and \(\ln U\) all describe the same consumer.

Common Utility Functions

Cobb-Douglas: \(U = q_1^a \, q_2^b\). Produces smooth, convex indifference curves. The consumer always buys some of both goods.

Perfect Substitutes: \(U = a \, q_1 + b \, q_2\). Straight-line indifference curves. The consumer views the goods as interchangeable (at rate \(a/b\)).

Perfect Complements (Leontief): \(U = \min(a \, q_1, \, b \, q_2)\). L-shaped indifference curves. The goods must be consumed in fixed proportions.

Mathematical Formalisation

The marginal utility of good \(i\) is the partial derivative of the utility function with respect to \(q_i\):

\[ MU_i = \frac{\partial U}{\partial q_i} \]

It measures the additional satisfaction from a small increase in the quantity of good \(i\), holding everything else constant. The law of diminishing marginal utility states that \(MU_i\) typically falls as \(q_i\) rises — the tenth slice of pizza is less satisfying than the first.

For Cobb-Douglas preferences \(U = q_1^{0.5} q_2^{0.5}\):

\[ MU_1 = 0.5 \, q_1^{-0.5} q_2^{0.5} = \frac{0.5 \sqrt{q_2}}{\sqrt{q_1}} \]

As \(q_1\) rises, \(MU_1\) falls — diminishing marginal utility in action.

4.3 The Budget Constraint

Intuition: What Can You Afford?

Preferences tell us what the consumer wants; the budget constraint tells us what they can have. With income \(Y\) and prices \(p_1\) and \(p_2\), the consumer can afford any bundle \((q_1, q_2)\) such that total spending does not exceed income:

\[ p_1 q_1 + p_2 q_2 \leq Y \]

The boundary of this set — where the consumer spends every penny — is the budget line:

\[ q_2 = \frac{Y}{p_2} - \frac{p_1}{p_2} q_1 \]

This is a straight line with a vertical intercept of \(Y/p_2\) (spend everything on good 2), a horizontal intercept of \(Y/p_1\) (spend everything on good 1), and a slope of \(-p_1/p_2\) (the rate at which the market allows you to swap good 1 for good 2).

Notice the effects: increasing income shifts the budget line outward (parallel shift — same slope, more affordable bundles). Increasing the price of good 1 rotates the line inward around the vertical intercept (you can buy less of good 1, but the maximum of good 2 is unchanged). The slope steepens because good 1 has become relatively more expensive.

Mathematical Formalisation

The budget line in our pizza-and-beer example with \(Y = 30\), \(p_1 = 5\), \(p_2 = 3\):

\[ q_2 = \frac{30}{3} - \frac{5}{3} q_1 = 10 - \frac{5}{3} q_1 \]

The slope is \(-p_1/p_2 = -5/3 \approx -1.67\). This means the opportunity cost of one pizza is 5/3 beers — the market rate at which the consumer can trade beer for pizza. The vertical intercept is 10 (buy only beer) and the horizontal intercept is 6 (buy only pizza).

The Slope Is the Opportunity Cost

The slope of the budget line, \(-p_1/p_2\), is the rate at which the market allows you to substitute one good for another. The MRS is the rate at which you are willing to substitute. The optimal choice is where these two rates are equal — we shall see this in the next section.

4.4 Optimal Consumer Choice

Intuition: The Best You Can Afford

The consumer wants to reach the highest possible indifference curve while staying within the budget constraint. Visually, imagine sliding the indifference curve outward from the origin until it just touches the budget line. That point of tangency is the optimal bundle.

At the optimum, the slope of the indifference curve (MRS) equals the slope of the budget line (price ratio). If they were not equal, the consumer could rearrange their spending and do better:

The Tangency Condition

If \(\text{MRS} > p_1/p_2\), the consumer values good 1 more than the market does. They should buy more of good 1 and less of good 2. If \(\text{MRS} < p_1/p_2\), the reverse. The optimal interior solution satisfies:

\[ \text{MRS} = \frac{MU_1}{MU_2} = \frac{p_1}{p_2} \]

or equivalently: \(\dfrac{MU_1}{p_1} = \dfrac{MU_2}{p_2}\) — the marginal utility per pound spent must be equal across all goods.

Mathematical Formalisation

For Cobb-Douglas preferences \(U = q_1 \cdot q_2\), the MRS is \(q_2/q_1\). The tangency condition is:

\[ \frac{q_2}{q_1} = \frac{p_1}{p_2} \quad \Rightarrow \quad q_2 = \frac{p_1}{p_2} q_1 \]

Substituting into the budget constraint \(p_1 q_1 + p_2 q_2 = Y\):

\[ p_1 q_1 + p_2 \cdot \frac{p_1}{p_2} q_1 = Y \quad \Rightarrow \quad 2 p_1 q_1 = Y \quad \Rightarrow \quad q_1^* = \frac{Y}{2p_1} \] \[ q_2^* = \frac{Y}{2p_2} \]

A beautiful result: with these preferences, the consumer spends exactly half their income on each good, regardless of prices. With \(Y = 30\), \(p_1 = 5\), \(p_2 = 3\):

\[ q_1^* = \frac{30}{2 \times 5} = 3, \quad q_2^* = \frac{30}{2 \times 3} = 5 \]

The optimal bundle is 3 pizzas and 5 beers, confirming our intuition from the opening example. The utility achieved is \(U^* = 3 \times 5 = 15\).

Example: A Price Increase

If the price of pizza rises to \(p_1 = 10\), the new optimum is \(q_1^* = 30/(2 \times 10) = 1.5\), \(q_2^* = 30/(2 \times 3) = 5\). The consumer halves their pizza consumption but their beer consumption is unchanged. With Cobb-Douglas preferences, a price change in one good does not affect the quantity of the other — the income share stays fixed at 50%.

More generally, for \(U = q_1^a q_2^b\), the optimal demands are:

\[ q_1^* = \frac{a}{a+b} \cdot \frac{Y}{p_1}, \qquad q_2^* = \frac{b}{a+b} \cdot \frac{Y}{p_2} \]

The consumer allocates a fraction \(a/(a+b)\) of income to good 1 and \(b/(a+b)\) to good 2. The exponents determine the budget shares.

4.5 Special Cases: Corner Solutions and Non-Standard Preferences

Intuition: When the Tangency Rule Breaks Down

The tangency condition works beautifully for smooth, convex indifference curves (like Cobb-Douglas). But some preferences produce solutions where the consumer buys only one good — a corner solution — rather than a mix.

Consider a consumer choosing between Coca-Cola and Pepsi. If they view them as identical, they will buy whichever is cheaper — all Coke or all Pepsi, never a mix. Their indifference curves are straight lines (perfect substitutes), and the tangency rule cannot apply because the budget line either has the same slope (any mix is optimal) or a different slope (corner solution).

Now consider left shoes and right shoes. Having 5 left shoes and 2 right shoes is no better than having 2 of each — the extra left shoes are useless. The goods must be consumed in fixed proportions (perfect complements), producing L-shaped indifference curves. The optimum is always at the kink of the L.

Mathematical Formalisation

Perfect Substitutes: \(U = a \, q_1 + b \, q_2\). The MRS is the constant \(a/b\). If \(a/b > p_1/p_2\), the consumer values good 1 more than the market does and buys only good 1: \(q_1^* = Y/p_1\). If \(a/b < p_1/p_2\), they buy only good 2. If \(a/b = p_1/p_2\), any point on the budget line is optimal.

For \(U = q_1 + q_2\) with \(p_1 = 5, p_2 = 3, Y = 30\): \(a/b = 1\) and \(p_1/p_2 = 5/3 \approx 1.67\). Since \(1 < 1.67\), good 2 gives more utility per pound. Solution: \(q_1^* = 0, \, q_2^* = 10\).

Perfect Complements: \(U = \min(a \, q_1, \, b \, q_2)\). The optimum is where \(a \, q_1 = b \, q_2\), i.e. \(q_2 = (a/b) q_1\). Substituting into the budget constraint:

\[ p_1 q_1 + p_2 \frac{a}{b} q_1 = Y \quad \Rightarrow \quad q_1^* = \frac{Y}{p_1 + p_2 (a/b)} \]

For \(U = \min(q_1, q_2)\) (one-to-one proportions) with \(p_1 = 5, p_2 = 3, Y = 30\):

\[ q_1^* = q_2^* = \frac{30}{5 + 3} = 3.75 \]

The consumer buys 3.75 of each, spending the entire budget to consume the goods in equal proportions.

Summary

In this chapter, we developed the theory of consumer choice — the framework that lies behind the demand curve.

Preferences are represented by indifference curves: bundles on the same curve give equal satisfaction, and higher curves are preferred. The slope of an indifference curve is the marginal rate of substitution (MRS), which typically diminishes as we move along the curve. A utility function assigns numbers to bundles in a way that respects the preference ordering, and the MRS equals the ratio of marginal utilities.

The budget constraint limits the consumer's choices. Its slope, \(-p_1/p_2\), reflects the market rate at which goods can be exchanged. Income changes shift the line; price changes rotate it.

The optimal choice is the point of tangency between the highest attainable indifference curve and the budget line, where \(\text{MRS} = p_1/p_2\). Equivalently, the marginal utility per pound must be equal across all goods. For Cobb-Douglas preferences, the consumer allocates fixed shares of income to each good.

Special cases require different solution methods: perfect substitutes typically produce corner solutions; perfect complements produce kink solutions at fixed proportions.
