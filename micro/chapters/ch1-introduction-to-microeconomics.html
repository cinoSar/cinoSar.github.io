The Problem: How Do You Decide?

You have 24 hours in a day. You could spend this afternoon revising for an exam, earning money at a part-time job, exercising, or relaxing with friends. You cannot do all four at once. Whatever you choose, you give up something else — and that sacrifice is the first and most important idea in economics. Every decision has a cost, even when no money changes hands.

Governments face the same problem at a vastly larger scale. In 2023, the UK Treasury had roughly £1.2 trillion to allocate across the NHS, schools, defence, infrastructure, welfare, and debt interest. Spending more on one item unavoidably means less for another. The question is not whether to make trade-offs, but how to make them well.

Microeconomics is the study of these choices: how individuals, households, and firms decide what to produce, consume, and trade, and how those decisions interact through markets to determine prices and quantities. The word “micro” comes from the Greek for “small”, but the questions are anything but small. They range from why a cup of coffee at a motorway service station costs three times as much as one at a supermarket, to how pharmaceutical firms decide whether to develop a new drug, to why housing in London is so expensive and what — if anything — policy can do about it.

Before tackling any of these questions, you need a set of analytical tools. This chapter introduces the four core principles that underpin everything that follows: scarcity and opportunity cost, marginal thinking, markets and prices, and incentives. Master these and the rest of the course is essentially a series of elaborations.

Key Insight

Economics is not primarily about money. It is about choice under scarcity: how decision-makers allocate limited resources among competing uses, and what consequences those allocations have for individuals and society. Money is just the most convenient unit of account for comparing alternatives.

1.1 Scarcity and Trade-offs

Opportunity Cost: The True Cost of Any Choice

Resources — time, money, land, labour, capital — are scarce relative to human desires. Scarcity forces choice. And every choice has an opportunity cost: the value of the next-best alternative foregone. If you spend £500 on a concert ticket, the opportunity cost is not just the £500 itself but whatever you would otherwise have done with it — perhaps a weekend trip to Edinburgh, or a month’s worth of groceries. If a government builds a new motorway, the opportunity cost is the hospitals, schools, or tax cuts that could have been funded instead.

Opportunity cost is not the same as accounting cost. An accounting cost is the monetary payment actually made. An opportunity cost is broader: it includes the value of any resource you already own but deploy in a particular way. An entrepreneur who quits a £60,000-per-year job to start a business incurs an accounting cost of zero in salary forgone — she simply stops receiving it — but an opportunity cost of £60,000. Getting this distinction right is crucial for understanding why rational agents sometimes appear to “leave money on the table”.

Opportunity Cost

The opportunity cost of a choice is the value of the best alternative sacrificed. It is always forward-looking (what could I do instead right now?) and never includes sunk costs (what did I already spend?). Rational decision-making requires comparing benefits against opportunity costs, not accounting costs.

The Production Possibility Frontier

The cleanest way to visualise scarcity and opportunity cost is the production possibility frontier (PPF). Consider a simple economy that can produce only two goods — say, smartphones and wheat — using fixed resources. The PPF traces out every efficient combination: all the points where you cannot produce more of one good without producing less of the other.

Points inside the frontier are inefficient — resources are wasted or idle. Points outside the frontier are unattainable given current resources and technology. Points on the frontier are efficient: you are getting the maximum possible output from available resources. Moving along the frontier from one point to another illustrates opportunity cost directly: to get more smartphones, you must give up some wheat.

The frontier is typically bowed outward (concave to the origin) because resources are not perfectly substitutable. Some resources are much better suited to growing wheat than assembling electronics, and vice versa. As you try to produce more and more smartphones by shifting resources away from wheat, you first pull the resources that are only marginally useful for wheat — cheap sacrifice — but eventually you must use resources that are highly productive in wheat and poorly suited to electronics. The opportunity cost of each additional smartphone therefore rises. This is the law of increasing opportunity cost.

Example: NHS Resource Allocation

The NHS faces a PPF in which the two “goods” are, say, cancer treatment and mental health services. With a fixed budget, spending more on cutting-edge oncology drugs means fewer psychiatric beds. The frontier bows outward because some clinical staff, equipment, and facilities are specialised: a radiotherapy machine cannot treat depression. The UK government’s 2023 decision to increase NHS spending on elective surgery backlogs implicitly moved the economy along this frontier, reducing resources available elsewhere.

1.2 Marginal Thinking

The Optimal Decision Rule

Most economic decisions are not all-or-nothing choices but questions of how much: how many hours to study, how many units to produce, how much to invest. The correct way to approach such questions is to think at the margin — to ask whether doing a little more of the activity is worthwhile.

The marginal benefit (MB) of an activity is the additional benefit from one more unit. The marginal cost (MC) is the additional cost of one more unit. Rational decision-making requires producing (or consuming, or doing) up to the point where MB equals MC. If MB > MC, do more: the extra benefit exceeds the extra cost. If MB < MC, do less: the extra cost exceeds the extra benefit. Only at MB = MC is there no profitable adjustment left.

The Fundamental Decision Rule

Choose the quantity \(q^*\) such that \(MB(q^*) = MC(q^*)\). At any quantity below \(q^*\), the marginal benefit exceeds the marginal cost and it is worth expanding. At any quantity above \(q^*\), the marginal cost exceeds the marginal benefit and it is worth contracting. This rule is equivalent to maximising net benefit (total benefit minus total cost).

Why Marginal Benefit Typically Falls and Marginal Cost Typically Rises

Marginal benefit typically decreases with quantity because of diminishing marginal utility: the first cup of coffee in the morning is wonderful; the fifth is barely worthwhile. Marginal cost typically increases with quantity because of diminishing marginal returns: as you add more of one input while others are fixed, each successive unit adds less output, meaning you need more input per unit of output. Together, these tendencies ensure that a unique, interior optimum MB = MC generally exists.

Common Error: Confusing Total and Marginal

It is tempting to keep doing an activity as long as the total benefit exceeds the total cost. But this can lead to producing too much. Even if total benefit exceeds total cost, if the marginal cost of the last unit exceeds its marginal benefit, net benefit would rise by reducing output. Always compare marginal quantities, not totals, at the decision margin.

1.3 Markets and Prices

The Price Mechanism

A market is any arrangement that brings buyers and sellers together to determine prices and quantities. Markets need not be physical places — a stock exchange, a classified-ad website, and a fish auction at Billingsgate all qualify. What they share is a price mechanism: a system by which the interaction of supply and demand generates prices that coordinate decentralised decisions.

When buyers want more of a good than sellers are willing to supply at the current price, the price is bid up. Higher prices make the good more attractive to produce (sellers expand supply) and less attractive to consume (buyers reduce demand), until quantity supplied equals quantity demanded at a new, higher equilibrium price. The reverse happens when supply exceeds demand: prices fall, discouraging production and encouraging consumption, until balance is restored.

This self-correcting mechanism — described by Adam Smith as the “invisible hand” — is one of the most remarkable properties of market economies. No central authority needs to calculate the right price; it emerges from the aggregate of millions of independent decisions. The price simultaneously signals scarcity (a high price tells producers to make more and consumers to economise) and provides an incentive to act on that signal.

Market Equilibrium

An equilibrium is a price-quantity pair \((P^*, Q^*)\) at which quantity demanded equals quantity supplied: \(Q_D(P^*) = Q_S(P^*)\). At any price above \(P^*\) there is a surplus (excess supply) that pushes prices down; at any price below \(P^*\) there is a shortage (excess demand) that pushes prices up. Chapter 2 develops this model in full; here we introduce its core logic.

1.4 Incentives

People Respond to Incentives

Perhaps the single most powerful principle in economics is that people respond to incentives. Change the costs or benefits of an action and behaviour will change in predictable directions. This principle is so robust that economists use it not just to explain market outcomes but to design policies, contracts, and institutions.

Incentives can be positive (a subsidy, a prize, a bonus) or negative (a tax, a fine, a reduced wage). They can be monetary or non-monetary (reputation, social approval, guilt). The key insight is that the relevant incentive is always the marginal one: not how much you gain or lose in total, but how much more you gain or lose from the next unit of the activity.

Consider road pricing. London’s congestion charge imposes a marginal cost on each additional car trip into the city centre. Some drivers whose MB from the trip is less than the charge switch to other modes; others continue driving. The charge does not ban cars from the city; it makes each car journey more expensive at the margin, changing the trade-off and therefore shifting behaviour. The same logic applies to carbon taxes, cigarette duties, and speed limits with fines.

Incentive Compatibility

A rule, contract, or policy is incentive-compatible if the people it governs find it in their self-interest to behave in the way the designer intends. Bad policy design ignores incentives and is undermined by rational self-interest. Good policy design aligns private incentives with social goals — a theme that runs through the entire course, from tax policy (Ch. 9) to contract design (Ch. 20).

Example: The Sugar Tax

The UK Soft Drinks Industry Levy (“sugar tax”), introduced in 2018, charges manufacturers £0.18–£0.24 per litre on drinks with high sugar content. Manufacturers faced a choice: pay the levy (raise consumer prices, reducing quantity demanded) or reformulate recipes to reduce sugar content (lowering or eliminating the levy). Both responses were rational. Remarkably, the reformulation response was substantial — the levy raised far less revenue than projected because firms changed behaviour rather than simply passing the cost on. This is the incentive principle in action: change the margin, change the decision.

1.5 The Formal Toolkit

Constrained Optimisation

The mathematical foundation of microeconomics is constrained optimisation: maximise (or minimise) an objective function subject to one or more constraints. Every major model in this course is a constrained optimisation problem in disguise. A consumer maximises utility subject to a budget constraint (Chapter 4). A firm maximises profit subject to a production technology (Chapter 6). A government maximises social welfare subject to a budget and information constraints (Chapters 9, 18, 19).

The general form is: choose \(x\) to maximise \(f(x)\) subject to \(g(x) \leq b\). The first-order condition (FOC) for an interior solution sets the derivative of the objective equal to zero (for unconstrained problems) or uses the Lagrangian method (for constrained problems). The Lagrangian is:

\[\mathcal{L}(x, \lambda) = f(x) - \lambda[g(x) - b]\]

The FOCs \(\partial \mathcal{L}/\partial x = 0\) and \(\partial \mathcal{L}/\partial \lambda = 0\) characterise the optimum. The multiplier \(\lambda\) has an important interpretation: it equals the marginal value of relaxing the constraint by one unit. If the budget constraint binds and \(\lambda = 5\), an extra £1 of budget raises the objective by £5 worth of utility.

Comparative Statics

Comparative statics is the technique of asking: how does the optimum change when an exogenous parameter changes? If the optimal quantity is \(q^* = (MB_0 - c_0) / 2k\) (where \(MB_0\) is the intercept of the MB curve, \(c_0\) is the base MC, and \(k\) governs the slope of MB), then:

\[\frac{\partial q^*}{\partial c_0} = -\frac{1}{2k} < 0\]

A rise in marginal cost reduces optimal quantity. This is a comparative statics result: it does not tell you what happens along the path from one equilibrium to another, only where the new equilibrium is. Comparative statics is the workhorse of applied economic analysis — it is how we derive testable predictions from models.

The Envelope Theorem

A powerful shortcut in comparative statics is the envelope theorem: the derivative of the maximised value of the objective with respect to a parameter equals the partial derivative of the original objective (not the Lagrangian) with respect to that parameter, evaluated at the optimum. Formally, if \(V(\alpha) = \max_x f(x, \alpha)\) and \(x^*(\alpha)\) is the optimising choice, then:

\[\frac{dV}{d\alpha} = \frac{\partial f(x^*(\alpha), \alpha)}{\partial \alpha}\]

The indirect effect through \(x^*\) drops out because of the FOC. This theorem reappears in producer theory (Hotelling’s lemma, Shephard’s lemma), consumer theory (Roy’s identity), and auction design.

Positive vs Normative Economics

A final methodological distinction: positive economics describes what is or what will be (“a sugar tax reduces consumption of sugary drinks”). Normative economics prescribes what ought to be (“the government should tax sugary drinks”). Models are tools for positive analysis; policy conclusions require normative judgements about whose welfare counts and how to weigh trade-offs. Both types of analysis are valuable, but conflating them — treating positive predictions as policy prescriptions without explicit value judgements — is one of the most common errors in applied economics.

Summary

Microeconomics rests on four principles that you will use in every chapter of this course. Scarcity forces choice, and every choice has an opportunity cost — the value of the next-best alternative. The production possibility frontier makes this trade-off visible and shows why opportunity costs typically rise as you specialise further. Marginal thinking — comparing the additional benefit and additional cost of one more unit — identifies the optimal level of any activity: produce up to the point where MB = MC, and stop there. Markets use the price mechanism to coordinate decentralised decisions, with prices simultaneously signalling relative scarcity and providing the incentive to act on it. And incentives work: changing the marginal cost or benefit of an action changes behaviour in predictable, exploitable ways.

The rest of this course develops, qualifies, and applies these principles. Chapter 2 builds the supply and demand model you previewed here into a complete analytical framework. Subsequent chapters extend it to consumer theory (Chapters 4–5), the firm (Chapters 6–9), strategic interaction (Chapters 13–14), and market failure (Chapters 18–20). The formal toolkit — constrained optimisation, comparative statics, the envelope theorem — provides the mathematical foundation throughout.

Keep returning to these four principles as the models grow more complex. If you find yourself lost in algebra, ask: what is being optimised? What is the constraint? What marginal trade-off is being equated? The answer will usually orient you. Economics at its best is not a collection of formulae to memorise but a coherent way of thinking about decisions, trade-offs, and the unintended consequences of well-meaning interventions.
