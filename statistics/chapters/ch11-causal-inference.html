11.1 — The Potential Outcomes Framework

Every method in this module is grounded in a single, powerful idea: the potential outcomes framework, also known as the Rubin Causal Model. It gives causal inference a rigorous mathematical language.

The Fundamental Problem of Causal Inference

For unit \(i\), define two potential outcomes:

  • \(Y_i(1)\): the outcome unit \(i\) would experience if treated
  • \(Y_i(0)\): the outcome unit \(i\) would experience if not treated

The individual treatment effect is:

\[\tau_i = Y_i(1) - Y_i(0)\]

This is the quantity we want. The problem: we only ever observe one potential outcome for each unit. A firm is either treated by a policy or it is not; a worker either participates in a training programme or they don't. We never observe the same unit in both states simultaneously. This is the fundamental problem of causal inference.

From Individual to Average Effects

Since \(\tau_i\) is unobservable, econometrics targets population averages:

\[\text{ATE} = \mathbb{E}[Y_i(1) - Y_i(0)]\]

This is the Average Treatment Effect (ATE): the average effect if we randomly assigned the entire population to treatment. Often, we care about a different estimand:

\[\text{ATT} = \mathbb{E}[Y_i(1) - Y_i(0) \mid D_i = 1]\]

The Average Treatment Effect on the Treated (ATT): the average effect among those who actually received treatment. In policy evaluation, ATT is usually the quantity of interest — we want to know whether the policy worked for the people it applied to.

Why Simple Comparisons Fail

A naive estimator compares treated and untreated units:

\[\mathbb{E}[Y_i \mid D_i = 1] - \mathbb{E}[Y_i \mid D_i = 0]\]

Expand this:

\[\underbrace{\mathbb{E}[Y_i(1) - Y_i(0) \mid D_i = 1]}_{\text{ATT}} + \underbrace{\mathbb{E}[Y_i(0) \mid D_i = 1] - \mathbb{E}[Y_i(0) \mid D_i = 0]}_{\text{Selection bias}}\]

The second term is selection bias: treated units may differ from untreated units in ways that affect outcomes regardless of treatment. Workers who enrol in job training may already be more motivated. Firms that adopt a new technology may already be more productive. The entire challenge of causal inference is to eliminate selection bias.

The Role of Research Design

Randomised controlled trials (RCTs) eliminate selection bias by construction: if assignment is random, \(\mathbb{E}[Y_i(0) \mid D_i = 1] = \mathbb{E}[Y_i(0) \mid D_i = 0]\). But RCTs are expensive, often infeasible, and sometimes unethical. The methods in this module — DiD, RDD, event studies — are quasi-experimental designs that exploit features of naturally occurring data to approximate the logic of a randomised experiment.

Key Concept: The Credibility Revolution

Since the 1990s, empirical economics has undergone a "credibility revolution": a shift away from structural models and OLS regressions towards research designs that credibly identify causal effects. The tools in this module — DiD, RDD, event studies — are the workhorses of modern applied microeconometrics. Understanding them is essential for reading and producing credible empirical research.

11.2 — Difference-in-Differences

The Intuition

Suppose a new environmental regulation takes effect in Region A but not Region B. We want to know whether the policy reduced pollution. A naive before-after comparison for Region A is confounded by other things that changed over time (economic conditions, weather). A naive treated-vs-control comparison is confounded by the fact that the regions may differ in baseline pollution levels.

Difference-in-Differences (DiD) solves both problems by taking two differences:

  1. First difference: within each region, the change in outcomes from before to after the policy.
  2. Second difference: the difference between the treated region's change and the control region's change.

The control region provides the counterfactual trend: what would have happened in the treated region absent the policy.

The DiD Estimand

With two periods (pre and post) and two groups (treated and control):

\[\hat{\tau}^{DiD} = (\bar{Y}_{T,\text{post}} - \bar{Y}_{T,\text{pre}}) - (\bar{Y}_{C,\text{post}} - \bar{Y}_{C,\text{pre}})\]

This can be rewritten as a 2×2 table:

Before After Difference Treated: Y_T0 Y_T1 (Y_T1 - Y_T0) Control: Y_C0 Y_C1 (Y_C1 - Y_C0) ───────────────────────────────────────────── DiD: (Y_T1 - Y_T0) - (Y_C1 - Y_C0)

DiD as a Regression

With more than two periods or multiple units, DiD is estimated as a regression. Let \(D_i\) be a dummy equal to 1 for treated units, \(Post_t\) a dummy equal to 1 for the post-treatment period, and their interaction \(D_i \times Post_t\) the DiD term:

\[Y_{it} = \alpha + \beta_1 D_i + \beta_2 Post_t + \beta_3 (D_i \times Post_t) + \varepsilon_{it}\]

The coefficient \(\beta_3\) is the DiD estimator: the differential change in outcomes for treated units relative to control units after treatment.

Equivalently, in a panel with unit and time fixed effects (as in Module 10):

\[Y_{it} = \alpha_i + \lambda_t + \beta_3 (D_i \times Post_t) + \varepsilon_{it}\]

The unit effects \(\alpha_i\) absorb all time-invariant unit differences; the time effects \(\lambda_t\) absorb all common time trends. The interaction \(\beta_3\) identifies the causal effect.

Simulating and Estimating DiD in Python

import numpy as np import pandas as pd import statsmodels.formula.api as smf np.random.seed(42) # Simulate DiD data: 100 treated firms, 100 control firms, 4 periods n_firms = 200 n_periods = 4 treatment_period = 2 # treatment occurs between period 2 and 3 true_effect = 5.0 firm_ids = np.repeat(np.arange(n_firms), n_periods) time_ids = np.tile(np.arange(n_periods), n_firms) treated = (firm_ids < 100).astype(int) # first 100 firms are treated post = (time_ids >= treatment_period).astype(int) # Firm fixed effects (unobserved heterogeneity) firm_fe = np.random.normal(0, 3, n_firms)[firm_ids] # Common time trend for all firms time_trend = time_ids * 2.0 # Treatment effect kicks in after treatment_period for treated firms treatment = true_effect * treated * post # Outcome Y = 20 + firm_fe + time_trend + treatment + np.random.normal(0, 2, n_firms * n_periods) df = pd.DataFrame({ 'firm_id': firm_ids, 'period': time_ids, 'treated': treated, 'post': post, 'Y': Y }) df['did_term'] = df['treated'] * df['post'] # Regression DiD with firm and time fixed effects model = smf.ols( 'Y ~ did_term + C(firm_id) + C(period)', data=df ).fit(cov_type='HC3') print(f"True treatment effect: {true_effect}") print(f"DiD estimated effect: {model.params['did_term']:.3f}") print(f"95% CI: [{model.conf_int().loc['did_term', 0]:.3f}, {model.conf_int().loc['did_term', 1]:.3f}]")

Key Concept: DiD Is Two-Way Fixed Effects

In a panel setting, DiD with multiple periods and units is numerically identical to a two-way fixed effects regression with a treatment indicator. The unit fixed effects absorb time-invariant differences; the time fixed effects absorb common trends; the treatment interaction identifies the causal effect. Everything you learned in Module 10 about two-way FE directly applies here.

11.3 — Parallel Trends & Validity Tests

The Parallel Trends Assumption

DiD requires a crucial identifying assumption: in the absence of treatment, treated and control units would have followed the same trend.

\[\mathbb{E}[Y_{it}(0) - Y_{it-1}(0) \mid D_i = 1] = \mathbb{E}[Y_{it}(0) - Y_{it-1}(0) \mid D_i = 0]\]

This is the parallel trends assumption. It does not require that treated and control units have the same levels — only that their trends would have been the same absent treatment.

Parallel trends cannot be directly tested for the post-treatment period (the counterfactual trend is unobserved). However, it can be assessed in the pre-treatment period, where we observe outcomes for both groups before any treatment occurs.

Pre-Trend Test

If treated and control units followed different trends before the treatment, it suggests they would also have diverged after treatment even without the intervention. This would invalidate the DiD estimate.

To test pre-trends formally, estimate a regression with period-by-treatment interactions for each pre-treatment period:

\[Y_{it} = \alpha_i + \lambda_t + \sum_{k \neq -1} \beta_k \cdot \mathbf{1}[t = t_0 + k] \cdot D_i + \varepsilon_{it}\]

where \(t_0\) is the treatment date and period \(-1\) (one period before treatment) is omitted as the normalisation. The \(\beta_k\) for \(k < 0\) are pre-treatment coefficients that should be close to zero if parallel trends holds. The \(\beta_k\) for \(k \geq 0\) trace out the post-treatment dynamic effects.

# Pre-trend test: interact treatment with each period dummy # Period 1 is omitted (reference), as it is one period before treatment df['period_str'] = 'p' + df['period'].astype(str) # Fully interacted model (treatment x time dummies) model_pretrend = smf.ols( 'Y ~ treated:C(period, Treatment(reference=1)) + C(firm_id) + C(period)', data=df ).fit(cov_type='HC3') # Extract the treatment x period interaction coefficients interaction_params = {k: v for k, v in model_pretrend.params.items() if 'treated' in k and 'period' in k} print("Treatment x Period interactions:") for k, v in sorted(interaction_params.items()): print(f" {k}: {v:.3f}") # Periods 0 should be near zero (pre-treatment periods = no effect) # Periods 2 and 3 should be positive and significant (treatment effect)

Pre-treatment coefficients near zero provide visual and statistical evidence that the parallel trends assumption is plausible. If pre-trends are non-zero and statistically significant, DiD is suspect.

Other Validity Concerns

Spillovers: If the treatment affects control units (e.g., a regional policy causes firms to relocate), the control group is contaminated and DiD over- or under-states the effect.

Anticipation effects: If treated units change behaviour before the official treatment date in anticipation of the policy, the estimated pre-trend may falsely show pre-existing divergence.

Compositional changes: If the composition of treated or control groups changes after treatment (e.g., some firms exit the market), comparisons become unreliable. Address this with a balanced panel or survival analysis.

Heterogeneous treatment timing (staggered DiD): If different units are treated at different times, the simple two-way FE DiD estimator can be misleading when treatment effects are heterogeneous. Recent literature (Callaway & Sant'Anna 2021; de Chaisemartin & D'Haultfœuille 2020) proposes robust alternatives. This is an active frontier in applied econometrics.

Key Concept: Parallel Trends Is Untestable Post-Treatment

Parallel trends is an identifying assumption — it cannot be proven, only made more or less plausible. Pre-trend tests in the pre-treatment period are the main tool for assessing its credibility. If treated and control units show different trends before treatment, DiD is not credible. If they show parallel pre-trends, it is suggestive but not conclusive. Strong research designs choose comparison groups with credibly parallel trends on theoretical grounds, not just empirically.

11.4 — Event Study Design

What Is an Event Study?

An event study is the dynamic version of DiD. Rather than estimating a single average post-treatment effect, it traces out how the effect evolves over time: how quickly does it materialise? Does it fade, grow, or remain constant? Are there pre-treatment anticipation effects?

Event studies are both a research design and a validity check. Plotting coefficients for pre-treatment and post-treatment periods on the same graph provides:

  • A visual pre-trend test (pre-treatment coefficients should be near zero).
  • An estimate of the dynamic treatment path (post-treatment coefficients reveal the time profile of effects).

The Event Study Regression

Let \(k\) be periods relative to the treatment date (negative = pre-treatment, zero = treatment period, positive = post-treatment). Omit \(k = -1\) as the reference period:

\[Y_{it} = \alpha_i + \lambda_t + \sum_{k=-K, k \neq -1}^{L} \beta_k \cdot \mathbf{1}[\text{Period}_{it} = k] \cdot D_i + \varepsilon_{it}\]

The \(\beta_k\) coefficients trace the treatment effect at each period relative to the event. Plotting these with confidence intervals is the event study plot.

import matplotlib.pyplot as plt # Create relative time variable (time since treatment) # Treatment occurs at period 2; reference period = 1 (k = -1 relative to treatment) df['rel_time'] = df['period'] - treatment_period # k = -2, -1, 0, +1 # Interact treatment with each relative-time dummy, omitting k=-1 event_formula = 'Y ~ ' interactions = [] for k in sorted(df['rel_time'].unique()): if k == -1: continue # reference period varname = f'rel_time_{k}'.replace('-', 'neg') df[varname] = ((df['rel_time'] == k) & (df['treated'] == 1)).astype(int) interactions.append(varname) event_formula = 'Y ~ ' + ' + '.join(interactions) + ' + C(firm_id) + C(period)' model_es = smf.ols(event_formula, data=df).fit(cov_type='HC3') # Extract coefficients and confidence intervals coef_names = interactions coefs = [model_es.params[v] for v in coef_names] ci_low = [model_es.conf_int().loc[v, 0] for v in coef_names] ci_high = [model_es.conf_int().loc[v, 1] for v in coef_names] # Reconstruct relative time axis (insert 0 for reference period) rel_times_plot = [-2, 0, 1] # k = -2, 0, +1 (k=-1 omitted, forced to 0) coefs_plot = coefs[:1] + [0] + coefs[1:] ci_low_plot = ci_low[:1] + [0] + ci_low[1:] ci_high_plot = ci_high[:1] + [0] + ci_high[1:] # Plot event study fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(8, 4)) ax.axhline(0, color='grey', linestyle='--', linewidth=1) ax.axvline(-0.5, color='black', linestyle=':', linewidth=1, label='Treatment onset') ax.errorbar( rel_times_plot, coefs_plot, yerr=[np.array(coefs_plot) - np.array(ci_low_plot), np.array(ci_high_plot) - np.array(coefs_plot)], fmt='o', capsize=4, color='steelblue', linewidth=2 ) ax.set_xlabel('Period Relative to Treatment') ax.set_ylabel('Treatment Effect Estimate') ax.set_title('Event Study Plot') ax.legend() plt.tight_layout() plt.show() print("Pre-treatment coefficients should be near zero (test of parallel trends)") print("Post-treatment coefficients show the dynamic treatment effect")

A well-executed event study plot has: coefficients near zero and statistically indistinguishable from zero for pre-treatment periods; a visible jump at or after the treatment date; and stable or growing post-treatment coefficients depending on the policy.

Key Concept: Event Study Plots Are Now Expected

In modern applied economics, simply reporting a DiD coefficient is no longer sufficient. Reviewers and readers expect an event study plot that shows the full dynamic pattern and demonstrates pre-trend plausibility. If you submit a DiD paper without an event study plot, expect a referee request for one. Build it into your workflow from the start.

11.5 — Regression Discontinuity Design

The Core Idea

Many real-world policies assign treatment based on a threshold rule: students above a test score threshold receive a scholarship; municipalities above a population cutoff receive extra funding; firms below a size threshold are exempt from a regulation.

Regression Discontinuity Design (RDD) exploits these threshold rules. Units just below the threshold are almost identical to units just above it — they differ mainly by chance — yet one group receives treatment and the other does not. This local randomness provides causal identification.

The Sharp RDD Setup

Let \(X_i\) be the running variable (also called the forcing variable) — the variable that determines treatment. Let \(c\) be the threshold. Treatment is assigned as:

\[D_i = \mathbf{1}[X_i \geq c]\]

The key assumption: units just below and just above \(c\) are comparable in all relevant characteristics, so any discontinuity in the outcome at \(c\) is caused by the treatment.

The RDD estimator is:

\[\hat{\tau}^{RDD} = \lim_{x \downarrow c} \mathbb{E}[Y_i \mid X_i = x] - \lim_{x \uparrow c} \mathbb{E}[Y_i \mid X_i = x]\]

This is the discontinuous jump in the conditional expectation of outcomes at the cutoff — estimated by fitting regressions on each side of the threshold.

Estimating RDD with Local Linear Regression

The modern approach: fit a separate local linear regression on each side of the cutoff, using only observations within a bandwidth \(h\) around the cutoff. Observations closer to the cutoff receive higher weights via a kernel function.

import numpy as np import pandas as pd import statsmodels.api as sm import matplotlib.pyplot as plt np.random.seed(99) # Simulate RDD data # Running variable: test score centred at threshold (0) # True treatment effect: 8 points n = 500 X = np.random.uniform(-1, 1, n) D = (X >= 0).astype(int) true_effect = 8 # Outcome: smooth function of X plus jump at threshold Y = 50 + 5 * X + true_effect * D + np.random.normal(0, 3, n) df_rdd = pd.DataFrame({'X': X, 'D': D, 'Y': Y}) # Estimate RDD: local linear regression within bandwidth bandwidth = 0.3 df_bw = df_rdd[np.abs(df_rdd['X']) <= bandwidth].copy() # Allow different slopes on each side (interact X with D) df_bw['X_center'] = df_bw['X'] df_bw['X_D'] = df_bw['X_center'] * df_bw['D'] X_mat = sm.add_constant(df_bw[['D', 'X_center', 'X_D']]) model_rdd = sm.OLS(df_bw['Y'], X_mat).fit() print(f"True treatment effect: {true_effect}") print(f"RDD estimated effect: {model_rdd.params['D']:.3f}") print(f"Standard error: {model_rdd.bse['D']:.3f}") print(f"95% CI: [{model_rdd.conf_int().loc['D', 0]:.3f}, {model_rdd.conf_int().loc['D', 1]:.3f}]") # Visualise the discontinuity fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(8, 4)) for side, color, label in [(0, 'steelblue', 'Control (X < 0)'), (1, 'tomato', 'Treated (X ≥ 0)')]: sub = df_rdd[df_rdd['D'] == side] ax.scatter(sub['X'], sub['Y'], alpha=0.3, s=10, color=color, label=label) ax.axvline(0, color='black', linestyle='--', linewidth=1, label='Cutoff') ax.set_xlabel('Running Variable (X)') ax.set_ylabel('Outcome (Y)') ax.set_title('Regression Discontinuity Design') ax.legend() plt.tight_layout() plt.show()

Identifying Assumptions and Validity Checks

No manipulation of the running variable: Units must not be able to sort themselves to just above (or below) the threshold. If firms can choose their reported size to qualify for an exemption, or students can appeal to shift their test score just above the cutoff, the local randomisation argument fails. The McCrary density test checks for a discontinuity in the density of the running variable at the threshold — a discontinuity suggests manipulation.

No other discontinuities at the cutoff: If other policies or variables also change at the same threshold, the RDD estimate conflates multiple effects. Check whether covariates are smooth at the cutoff (run the same regression using pre-determined covariates as the outcome — their coefficients should be near zero).

Bandwidth selection: The bandwidth \(h\) determines the sample used for estimation. A smaller bandwidth gives observations more similar to each other (less bias) but fewer observations (more variance). The optimal bandwidth is chosen to minimise mean squared error; the rdrobust package (available in R and Python) implements data-driven bandwidth selection.

Local Average Treatment Effect

RDD identifies the treatment effect only for units near the threshold — the Local Average Treatment Effect (LATE) at the cutoff. This may not generalise to units far from the threshold. Whether this is a limitation depends on the research question: if we care about whether a scholarship programme works for marginal applicants (those near the cutoff), LATE is exactly what we want.

Key Concept: RDD Has High Internal but Limited External Validity

RDD produces highly credible causal estimates for units near the threshold, because local randomisation is close to actual randomisation. This is strong internal validity. But the estimate applies only to units near the threshold, limiting external validity — you cannot readily extrapolate to the full population. This is the fundamental trade-off of all quasi-experimental designs: the more credible the identification, the more local the estimate.

11.6 — Choosing a Research Design

The Hierarchy of Evidence

Not all research designs are equally credible. The closer a design comes to randomised assignment, the more credibly it identifies causal effects. In practice, the right design depends on what variation is available in the data.

Most credible ────────────────────────────────── Least credible RCT Quasi-experiments OLS with controls │ (DiD, RDD, IV) │ │ │ │ Random Exploit natural Hope covariates assignment experiments, control for all policy rules confounders

Matching the Design to the Data

You have a policy that affected some units but not others, with data before and after: Use DiD. Assess parallel trends in the pre-period. Plot event study coefficients.

You have a threshold rule that determines treatment (test score, income cutoff, firm size): Use RDD. Check for manipulation (McCrary test). Verify covariate continuity at the cutoff. Use local linear regression with optimal bandwidth.

You have an exogenous variable that affects treatment but not outcomes directly: Use instrumental variables (Module 09). Verify relevance and argue for exclusion restriction.

You have panel data with time-varying treatment and no natural experiment: Use two-way fixed effects (Module 10). Acknowledge that time-varying confounders remain a threat.

You have a cross-section and a natural experiment is unavailable: Use observational regression with controls, but be honest about its limitations. Consider matching or propensity score methods to make treated and control groups more comparable.

Reporting Standards

A complete causal analysis should always include:

  1. A clear statement of the identifying assumption — what must be true for the estimate to be causal?
  2. A visual check — event study plot for DiD, discontinuity plot for RDD, first stage for IV.
  3. Robustness checks — alternative bandwidths (RDD), alternative control groups (DiD), alternative specifications.
  4. Discussion of threats — what violations of the identifying assumption are most plausible, and how serious are they?

Key Takeaways

  • The potential outcomes framework defines causal effects rigorously. The individual treatment effect \(\tau_i = Y_i(1) - Y_i(0)\) is unobservable; we target population averages (ATE, ATT) using research designs that credibly eliminate selection bias.
  • DiD exploits before-after variation in treated and control groups. It differences out both time-invariant unit differences and common time trends, identifying the causal effect under the parallel trends assumption.
  • Parallel trends is the key DiD assumption. It cannot be directly tested post-treatment, but pre-trend tests provide evidence of its plausibility. Always plot event study coefficients showing the full dynamic treatment path.
  • Event studies are the modern standard for DiD analysis. They visualise pre-trends (validity check) and post-treatment dynamics simultaneously. A good event study plot is expected in any serious DiD paper.
  • RDD exploits threshold rules for treatment assignment. Units near the cutoff are locally comparable; the jump in outcomes at the threshold identifies the causal effect. Key validity checks are the McCrary density test (no manipulation) and covariate continuity.
  • RDD estimates are local. The estimate applies only to units near the threshold (LATE at cutoff). This is high internal validity but limited external validity.
  • Design choice depends on what variation is available. Match the research design to the natural experiment at hand — DiD for policy roll-outs, RDD for threshold rules, IV for exogenous instruments, Panel FE for time-varying within-unit variation.
  • Always state, test, and discuss your identifying assumption. The credibility of a causal estimate rests entirely on the credibility of its identifying assumption. A careful researcher makes this explicit, tests it where possible, and honestly discusses threats.
